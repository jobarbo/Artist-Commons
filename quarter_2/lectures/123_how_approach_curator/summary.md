# How to Approach Curators and Galleries

---

## Overview

Artists who build lasting opportunities tend to invest in **community** over transactional networking. Warm connections beat cold outreach; curiosity beats pitching; and the goal of email or events is usually **the next conversation**, not closing a deal on the first contact. Outreach parallels **job applications**: align materials, research targets, tailor contact, track relationships, and evaluate opportunities two-way—not every gallery or invitation deserves a yes. Topics covered: warm introductions, in-person presence, research, materials, email structure, follow-up, studio visits, readiness, and red flags.

---

## 1. Community vs. Networking

- Best opportunities come from **relationships**, not one-off asks
- **Warm** beats **cold**: meet before you ask, or get a **warm introduction** from a mutual contact
- You can also "create warmth" by showing up after talks, openings, residencies, crits, open studios, or when someone has already engaged with your work (in person or online)
- Networking is gradual **professional familiarity**; it feels extractive when you only appear when you want something
- Core stance: be a **curious** person genuinely interested in the curator's work—not manipulation

---

## 2. Warm Introductions and Early Follow-Ups

### Example warm intro (fictional)

A third party emails curator Sarah after LACMA: introduces sculptor **Cameron**, ties Cameron to Sarah's past show (*Transient Territory*—fictional name), repeats the artist's name, gives concrete practice words ("abstracts in found wood"), attaches **one photo**, notes Cameron's Venice studio (second "off ramp"), keeps tone casual.

> Hi Sarah, it was great seeing you at the LACMA opening. I want to introduce you to Cameron, the sculptor I was telling you about. They're asking the kinds of questions you've been interested in with your curation work over the last few years, especially the *Transient Territory* show last year. I have a feeling you'll really dig their newest body of abstracts in found wood. I'm attaching one photo below from a studio visit I did with them last month. Cameron's studio is based in Venice Beach, so not far from you if you want to check it out. Hopefully I'll see you next week at Frieze. Warmly, [mutual contact]

**What works:**

- Point of reference ("great seeing you at LACMA")—not a random contact
- **Specific fit** with the curator's interests (not "you should meet this artist")
- **Reinforce** key details (busy readers skim)
- **Low pressure**: one image; curator or artist can take the next step
- Multiple paths: reply for more info, or visit studio locally

### Warm follow-ups (no big ask yet)

- After a talk: thank them, reference something they shared (e.g. Lee Krasner letters), ask for a reading recommendation
- After social engagement: share unpublished next work in a series—"secret knowledge," helpful not demanding
- **Start going to art events now**, even before you feel "ready" for studio visits

---

## 3. In-Person Presence

- Go to exhibitions; attend **curator talks** tied to shows; say hi briefly afterward
- **Don't monopolize** time at openings (anecdote: someone told a Sotheby's director the show was "shit" and pushed a portfolio)
- **Repeated encounters** matter more than one meeting
- Be curious about the community, current art, and differences between galleries
- People who only show up when they want something are "boring and annoying"

---

## 4. How to Open: Circles and Introductions

- Join circles where you know **at least one** person; ask for a proper intro that connects **why you're relevant** to each other
- **Types gravitate together**: artists, curators/gallery staff, collectors—bridge circles to grow your network
- **Be specific**, not "I'm an artist": medium + 1–2 memorable hooks (e.g. "I draw things with robots")
- People remember **one to two things** about you—choose what to promote in that moment (e.g. when curating a platform, lead with that role only; other practices can wait)
- **Don't overshare** or monologue (Silicon Valley "always pitching" trap)
- Speak in **1–3 sentences** that invite "tell me more"; workshop lines like a comedian tests material on the road
- **Musician analogy:** touring bands give a band name in the elevator and move on—no genre lecture. Unusual hooks ("I draw things with robots") give curious listeners more to ask about than generic "I'm a guitarist"

> It is okay to share a little and not ask for more—not everyone will care in the elevator, and that's fine.

## 5. Research and Curator vs. Gallerist

### Before outreach

- Research: what they show (aesthetic, conceptual, material fit); emerging vs. mid-career vs. established; submissions policy; independent vs. institutional curator; **mutual connections**
- **Relationship before opportunity** in most cases
- Strategy: identify **15–20 genuinely aligned** galleries/curators (Chet Holmes "Dream 100" idea—start smaller), prepare materials, tailored outreach in batches, **tracker**, keep showing up

### Function, not title

"Curator" is used loosely (accurate, aspirational, commercial prestige). Ask: **what role do they play** with artists, exhibitions, objects, and money?

| Functioning mainly as **curator** | Functioning mainly as **gallerist/dealer** |
|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Thematic/research-driven shows | Roster, fairs, collectors |
| Project-based relationship | Longer-term business relationship |
| Frames work in discourse | Sells/places work, editions, prices |
| Not primarily selling | Keeps lights on; "close the deal" |

Same art-history talk can serve different goals (historical argument vs. sale). Many small spaces **blend** roles—judge **center of gravity**.

**Studio-visit mindset differs:** dealer wants sellable, consistent, professional work now; curator asks fit for exhibition idea, conversation among works, conceptual reason to include **now**.

## 6. Materials Packet (Before the "Job Application")

Organize before outreach:

- Website, bio, statement
- **8–15 strong images**; image list (what's available for sale—dealers ask)
- CV when it won't feel thin (shows, residencies, awards, education if relevant)
- Short email intro (workshopped curiosity lines)
- PDF portfolio (can include more framing than JPEGs alone)

**Edit quality over quantity**—too much signals insecurity; tailor length to the bandwidth you'd want in your inbox.

### Outreach map homework

For each of 15–20 contacts: category (gallery / curator / project space), **why fit**, appropriate channel (email, social, text), **cold vs. warm**, mutual contacts for possible intro.

## 7. Email Goals and Anatomy

### Goals (often sequential)

- Introduce work or share an update
- Request studio visit or portfolio review
- Continue a prior conversation
- Ask a **context-tied** question
- Show curiosity—not close a deal immediately

> The goal is not to close the deal; it's to get to the **next conversation**.

### Strong email structure

1. Clear **subject line**
2. Professional greeting (avoid "Hey" for strangers—**Hi** / **Hello** OK)
3. One sentence: **why them specifically** (you've seen their shows/spaces)
4. **1–3 sentences** on practice (concrete, workshopped)
5. **Gentle ask** (open to studio visit? OK to send portfolio? know other curators for this theme?)
6. Link or selected work; short close

If a warm contact is locked on a multi-year institutional project, a portfolio conversation can still yield **referrals** to other curators or project spaces where your themes fit.

**Tone:** professional, specific, human (not ChatGPT); warm not overly familiar; confident not inflated; no jargon bath or excessive flattery; not needy. Bridge the gap between conversational studio talk and jargon-heavy artist statements—elevate how you speak about work without sounding academic in email.

### Emails that fail

- Indiscriminate blast; form-letter / AI tone; generic "I love your work"
- Too much biography; too many attachments; attachments without context
- Over-explaining instead of letting work speak
- **Ask too big too soon** (solo show, representation)

> Weak outreach: "please notice me." Strong outreach: "I believe there's a relevant point of connection here."

### Follow-up types

1. **Administrative** — confirm visit, send promised PDF/image  
2. **Relational** — thank-you after real exchange  
3. **Continuity** — share something tied to prior talk  
4. **Speculative** — brief reappearance after cold outreach (sparingly)

**Best follow-up has a reason**—new context, not "did you see my email?" Avoid repeated nudges, impatience, making them manage your anxiety. Silence may mean no, not now, busy, or buried—don't over-read. Use a **tracker** (Airtable, spreadsheet, Notes).

> **Selling is helping** — pass information you think fits their taste (a horror-movie recommendation, an image that reminds them why they do the job).

**Follow-up timing:** one thoughtful second nudge if something was buried; then a long break. From the curator's side of the inbox: after a second message with no reply, waiting **up to a year** before reaching out again is normal unless you reconnect in person—busy people receive thousands of requests.

Leave **something for later** at openings (don't resolve every talking point)—gives a natural reason to reappear.

## 8. Studio Visits

### What it is

Scheduled conversation: visitor builds a mental map of work for their **specific job** (sell vs. program). They're often **rooting for you** to solve their calendar hole—not grading you as an enemy.

### Prepare by audience

- **Dealer:** strong recent **unsold** work; coherence; de-risk their investment (words, money, staff, fairs)  
- **Curator:** breadth, through-line, ideas, process, journey  

- Edit what's visible; remove distracting clutter; plan **arc** (beginning / middle / end as host)
- Sentences ready on process, influences, stakes, current questions—but **improv** from their reactions
- Anticipate questions from their writing and recent posts
- **Physical:** drinks, seating, movement through space, noise/temperature, practical handouts
- **Leave material for post-visit follow-up**—don't hand everything at the door

### Studio visit don'ts

- Show everything; over-explain before they look; apologize constantly; get defensive
- **Dealers:** don't display work already sold but not yet shipped—they may read that as a missed opportunity
- Pressure for immediate representation, show, or consignment

### After the visit

- Thank-you; mention 1–2 real points from conversation; send promised items promptly
- Reflect: recurring questions, strong vs. muddy explanations, what resonated, what to revise

> Every studio visit is a win if you treat it as learning from an expert eye, not only as a transaction.

## 9. When Are You Ready?

Ask: **Am I ready to be seen this clearly?**—not only "how do I get a solo show?"

**Warmth matters:** closer contacts can see earlier work and return later; cold big-name curators may not offer a second chance if you're not ready.

**Premature outreach signs:**

- Scattered/weak body of work; messy website/socials as archive not portfolio
- Can't talk about work yet (haven't workshopped intro sentences)
- Best work still ahead; chasing milestones instead of **relationships**

Start with **friends** for practice visits; don't go "too big too soon." A **portfolio review over Zoom** with a warm contact is a low-stakes way to practice before inviting a cold big-name curator.

---

## 10. Discernment and Red Flags

- **Attention ≠ legitimacy**; not every opportunity deserves yes
- Evaluate them in the studio too—you may need to work together **six+ months**
- Red flags: vague promises, pressure tactics, poor communication on factual asks, exploitative requests, disrespect/disorganization
- Don't let hunger for validation override judgment
- Gallery representation is **not** a lottery ticket—research what they actually provide (marketing, communication, nurturing)

---

## 11. Career Stage and Non-Linearity

| Stage | Outreach emphasis |
|-------|-------------------|
| Emerging | Visibility, conversation, local familiarity |
| Relocating | Build local community first |
| Medium change | Reposition narrative—through line or rupture? |
| More established | Project-specific; curator relationships often one-off at large institutions |

Outreach is a **sequence** over months/years: awareness → research → attend → connect → sustain → deepen. Reward of success is **more community**, not only sales—must genuinely enjoy the scene you join.

---

## Homework

- Build **outreach map**: 15–20 aligned contacts with fit notes, channel, warm/cold, mutuals
- Workshop **1–3 sentence** introductions that invite follow-up questions
- Start attending events and bridging circles even before "big" outreach

---

## Takeaways

- **Community and curiosity** over transactional networking  
- **Warm, specific, low-pressure** contact; goal = next conversation  
- Distinguish **curator** vs. **gallerist** by function; tailor visit and materials  
- **Research + tracker + edited packet** before blasting inboxes  
- Studio visits: host an arc, listen, follow up with reason—not desperation  
- Build **durable professional relationships**; evaluate opportunities both ways
