à# The Crit — Sarah Thornton, _Seven Days in the Art World_ (Ch. 2)

**Summary of Chapter 2: "The Crit"**

Thornton spends a full day at the California Institute of the Arts (CalArts) auditing **Michael Asher**’s legendary **Post-Studio** critique class. The chapter follows three MFA students—**Josh**, **Hobbs**, and **Fiona**—as they present work in a marathon session that runs from 10 A.M. past midnight. Along the way, Thornton weaves in the history of the crit at CalArts, interviews with faculty and critics (including **John Baldessari**, **Mary Kelly**, **Dave Hickey**, **Leslie Dick**, **Thomas Lawson**, and **Chris Burden**), and reflections on what artists learn in art school, how identity is formed, and how the crit contrasts with the market-driven art world.

---

## Overview

"The Crit" takes the reader inside **F200**, a windowless CalArts classroom where Asher has run his crit since 1974. A **crit** is a seminar in which student-artists present work for collective critique. Thornton is allowed to observe but not speak, so as not to disturb "the chemistry." The day unfolds as a **twelve-plus-hour ritual**: morning session on Josh’s drawings, lunch break (including a ride to Whole Foods with students), afternoon discussion of Fiona’s _Painting Room II_, and an evening session focused on Hobbs’s photographs and the western genre. The chapter argues that the dynamics of the crit are central to understanding how the art world works—not as market or spectacle, but as a site where artists learn to "occupy" the name "artist" and where debate, endurance, and peer culture matter as much as any single verdict on the work.

## Setting and Format

### CalArts and F200

- **CalArts** sits in Valencia, in a building that feels like an "underground bunker" away from the Southern California sun. F200 has cement walls, fluorescent light, thin carpet, forty chairs, four tables, two chalkboards, and a lone jumbo beanbag.
- The room echoes **Asher’s own practice**: institutional critique, minimal presence, "functional" spaces that prompt communication. His work is often invisible or ephemeral (e.g., removing a gallery wall to expose the office; a free catalogue of MoMA deaccessions in the gift shop). He has no dealer and his art "lives on in anecdotes"; his goal is "to animate debate."
- **Post-Studio** was founded by **John Baldessari** in 1970 (when CalArts opened) as an alternative to "Conceptual Art"—narrow—in favor of a name that embraced everyone who didn’t make traditional art. Baldessari now teaches at UCLA but remains associated with the "think-tank" model that CalArts embodies.

### The Three Presenters

- **Josh**: beard, baseball cap, jeans; presents large drawings from a black portfolio. His crit drifts toward political activism and the viability of politics in his work; at one point he says he wants to "put the group to work" on that question.
- **Fiona**: evokes "the ghost of Frida Kahlo"—long green skirt, red hibiscus in her hair. She shows _Painting Room II_, an installation where paint spills off canvases onto wall and floor (Twombly-like scribbles, Pollock-like drips, a desk evoking _A Room of One’s Own_). She describes a "schizophrenic practice" (sociopolitical work plus painting) and insists she made formal decisions only, not "critical" ones, and wanted to revisit abstraction and "the poetics of space."
- **Hobbs**: slim, tomboy, pink T-shirt and jeans. She presents three color photographs (horse by a tree, cowboys about to duel, stuntman falling onto a mattress in the desert). Her themes: photography as "violent tool," the western genre, the absurd, and a confession that she can identify with the conflict in Thomas Mann’s line that "all women are misogynists"—pleasure in stereotypes even when she knows they’re wrong. She values humor as "corporeal and crass."

### Rituals and Snacks

- Presenting students bring **snacks** (Safeway cola, cookies, mini-muffins, grapes) as an "acknowledged peace offering." Later: pizza, Hershey’s Kisses, dogs in the room, pillows and blankets. The class runs so long that people nap, leave for calls, and return; by evening, partners and pets swell the room.

## What Is a Crit? Contrasting Models

### Student-Led, Instructor as Witness

- In many MFA programs, a crit is **five experts** telling the student what they think. At L.A. art schools, **students do most of the talking** while instructors "bear witness." The ideal is a "utopian" focus on understanding the work as deeply as possible—though crits can also feel like cross-examinations that "tear apart" the artist.
- Thornton stresses the contrast with the market: crits are the opposite of the "five-second glance and shallow dollar values" at auctions and fairs.

### Dave Hickey: Against Group Crits

- **Dave Hickey** refuses to run group crits. He describes his style as "Uncle Buck—Hey, smoke this." His view: group crits are "social occasions that reinforce the norm," "impose a standardized discourse," and "privilege unfinished, incompetent art." He tells students, "If you’re not sick, don’t call the doctor," and says, "I don’t care about an artist’s intentions. I care if the work looks like it might have some consequences."

### Mary Kelly: The Artist Doesn’t Speak

- **Mary Kelly** (feminist conceptualist, Goldsmiths, CalArts, UCLA) runs an **alternative critique** where the **only person not allowed to speak is the presenting artist**. Her motto: "Never go to the wall text. Never ask the artist. Learn to read the work." Art produces arguments; asking the artist for words is "just a parallel discourse," and artists often don’t fully understand what they’ve made. Her method: phenomenological openness first ("like yoga… empty your mind and be receptive"), then deciphering the "concrete signifying material." She stops when interpretation might be "going too far."

### William E. Jones: Defending Intention-Based Crits

- **William E. Jones** (filmmaker, Asher’s former student and occasional substitute) argues that crits that **interrogate intentions** prepare students for professional life: "negotiating interviews, conversations with critics, press releases, catalogues, and wall texts are part of the responsibility of the artist." Being put on the spot builds "thick skins" and helps students see criticism as "rhetoric rather than personal attack." Crucially, they need to find a "non-negotiable core" that will carry them through "a forty-year artistic practice."

### Howard Singerman: Learning to Be an Artist

- **Howard Singerman** (_Art Subjects_): the most important thing art school teaches is "how to be an artist, how to occupy that name, how to embody that occupation." The **crit** is where that identity is tried out in "semipublic" ground—even if full comfort with the title "artist" often comes only later (dealer, museum show, teaching job).

## Key Themes in the Chapter

### MFA as Legitimator

- Since the 1960s, the **MFA** has become the "first legitimator" in an artist’s career, followed by awards, representation, press, collections, museum shows, biennials, and auction interest. Résumés of artists under fifty in major international shows routinely list MFAs from a small set of selective schools.
- **L.A.** is described as a magnet: strong programs (CalArts, UCLA, Art Center, USC, Otis), cheaper rent, climate, and distance from New York foster risk-taking. Teaching is not stigmatized; many see it as part of their "practice," and a full-time contract is a "credibility-enhancer."

### Breaking Down and "Wobbling"

- **Leslie Dick** (writer on the art faculty): "Why come to grad school? It’s about paying a lot of money so you can change. Whatever you thought was certain about how to make art is dismantled. You wobble. You don’t make any sense at all. That’s why you are here." She adds that faculty can be "complacent about the pain" because they’ve seen it so often; "everything goes to pieces in the first year and it comes together in the second."
- **Falling apart** in a crit is framed as an expected part of CalArts pedagogy rather than pure shame.

### "What Is an Artist?"

- Thornton’s question "What is an artist?" provokes strong reactions—shock, "That’s not fair!," and from a senior figure, an accusation of being "stupid." A curator calls it "almost tautological… an artist is someone who makes art."
- **Leslie Dick**: The work of the artist is "play, but play in the most serious sense… You are materializing—taking something from the inside and putting it out into the world so you can be relieved of it."
- **Thomas Lawson** (dean, painter): "It’s not necessarily someone who sells a bunch of objects through a fancy gallery. An artist thinks about culture through visual means." He came to CalArts as dean partly to "reinvest the visual" after a period when MFAs were praised while "doing no visible work": "Ideas are a dime a dozen. You’ve got to put it into some sort of form."

### Criticality vs. Creativity

- **Criticality** (probed with **Charles Gaines**): "a strategy for the production of knowledge." The CalArts view: "art should interrogate the social and cultural ideas of its time" rather than merely produce pleasure or feelings.
- **Creativity** is a "dirty word" in Post-Studio—"people would gag"; it’s associated with "beautiful," "sublime," "masterpiece," and an "essentialist" genius myth. Thornton suggests creativity may be the "unteachable core" that admissions looks for; **Steven Lavine** (president): "We hunt for students who have some spark of originality. It might seem like eccentricity or cussedness."
- **Thomas Lawson**: "We are looking for the kind of kids who didn’t quite fit in at high school." High academic achievement can be a "contra-indicator."

### Painting at CalArts

- CalArts has a reputation for being "inhospitable" to painting. Lawson: "The beef against us is that we are not an emotional painterly type of school. That’s true. But we have a system that is open and intelligent… You can do anything you want, if you can defend it." For the "taciturn painter," though, "painting is not about talking"—skill and mistake are close, and it’s "difficult to defend." Fiona’s _Painting Room II_ is explicitly a reassertion of the studio and of formal, non-"critical" painting in that context.
- Early CalArts mantra: "No technique before need"; the joke was that some schools taught "up to the wrist" (craft) while CalArts taught "down to the wrist" (conceptual).

### Asher’s Pedagogy: Minimal Speech, Maximum Time

- Asher speaks very little. His first words in the morning, after long silence, are to Josh: "Why didn’t you enter the project through language or music?" Later he asks Josh to "put the group to work" and eventually, "What do you want, Josh?"
- He treats **time** as practical, not theoretical: "For clear investigations, you need time. That is the only rule of thumb. If you don’t have it, you run the risk of being superficial." He doesn’t remember how the class got so long—"People had more to say."
- **Hirsch Perlman**: Asher "personally underwrites the hours after 5 P.M.," and students "line up" to spend them there. "It’s one thing to put in the unpaid hours, it’s another to get the students to do that."
- One of Asher’s favorite words: **workable**. The importance of the class may have to do with its "spirit of production." **Exhaustiveness** and **endurance** are thematized—when "there is nothing to say, that becomes the question."

## Notable Moments and Quotes

### Baldessari

- "Art comes out of failure… You have to try things out. You can’t sit around, terrified of being incorrect, saying, ‘I won’t do anything until I do a masterpiece.’"
- On knowing when he’s run a great crit: "You don’t know. Quite often when I thought I was brilliant, I wasn’t. Then when I was really teaching, I wasn’t aware of it."
- "Students need to see that art is made by human beings just like them."

### Chris Burden and the UCLA Gun Incident

- In a UCLA crit, a student in a dark suit and red tie pulled a gun, loaded a silver bullet, spun the chamber, pointed it at his own head, and pulled the trigger (it clicked). He fled, shots were heard outside, then he returned. The crit continued with a tearful discussion. The act was a twisted homage/parody of **Chris Burden**’s 1971 _Shoot_ (he had himself shot in the arm in a gallery).
- Burden (not teaching that class): the student "should have been expelled on the spot" and "violated about five rules." The dean of student affairs "thought it was all theater." Burden resigned after twenty-six years: "I do not want to be part of this insanity."
- He contrasts art—"magic and alchemy," "trust your own intuition"—with academia’s "rational group-think" and suspicion of "the guy who stirs the big black pot."

### Post-Crit Car Conversation

- Students debate a vocal classmate: "He’s so arrogant and patronizing… ‘I don’t understand’ really means ‘You are an idiot.’" Another: "He’s overinstitutionalized, domineeringly PC, and macho… He got ripped to shreds when he had his crit."
- On Asher: "He certainly gives you enough rope to hang yourself"; "Michael is so minimal and abstract that sometimes I think he might dematerialize before our very eyes"; "He should wear a lab coat."

### Fiona After Her Crit

- "You go in and out of consciousness. When so many people open up your work, they say things that you never imagined, and you start to feel baffled."
- "To get the most out of your crit, you have to have a mysterious blend of complete commitment to your decisions and total openness to reconsider everything."
- "Students make work just because it stands up well in critiques, but outside the classroom it is often inconsequential."

### Market and Career

- **Hirsch Perlman**: "The art market simmers underneath all of these schools. Every student thinks that he can jumpstart his career by being in one of these programs. But nine out of ten times the student is in for a big surprise, and nobody wants to talk about it."
- **Mary Kelly**: She used to find it depressing that so few students become full-time artists; now she thinks "it is not sad at all. I believe in education for its own sake, because it is deeply humanizing."
- **Steven Lavine**: "We’re idealistic. We don’t prepare students to do jobs that already exist. Our mission is to help every student develop a voice of his or her own… At CalArts, people want to make work that has a relationship to what is under discussion rather than what is hot for sale at the moment."

### Good Artist, Endurance, and Success

- **Paul Schimmel** (MOCA): "Talent is a double-edged sword. What you are given is not really yours. What you work at, what you struggle for, what you have to take command of—that often makes for very good art."
- **Michael Craig-Martin** (Goldsmiths, YBAs): "For art students, the people who matter most are the peer group." Artists need "friendships with an inbuilt critique." "All the Renaissance artists knew their contemporaries. So did the impressionists… The cubists were not simply individual geniuses."
- **Hobbs** (in the crit): "The art world is like a western—full of cowboys, whores, and dandies." Robert Smithson, Bruce Nauman, James Turrell as frontier figures.

## Takeaways

- The **Post-Studio crit** is portrayed as **Asher’s greatest and most influential work**: a decades-long institutional critique that "reveals the limits of the rest of the curriculum," a "sound piece" with Asher at the "quiet eye of a multivocal storm," and a "minimalist performance" of listening and occasional throat-clearing.
- **Crits** are where artists learn to "occupy" the name artist, develop thick skin, find a "workable" core, and form peer alliances (as with the YBAs at Goldsmiths). They can be utopian (collective focus on understanding) or painful (cross-examination, wobbling, breakdown).
- **CalArts** is presented as idealistic, left-leaning in rhetoric ("center-left" in practice, per Lavine), and deliberately distant from preparing students for existing jobs or market trends—while the market "simmers underneath" and students are "dying to know" how careers actually work.
- The chapter frames the crit as a **counterpoint to the market**: a space where time, exhaustion, and debate matter more than the "five-second glance" and "shallow dollar values" of auctions and fairs—and as vital to understanding "the way the art world works."
