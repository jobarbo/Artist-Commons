# Philosophy of Art: What Am I Making? Part 2

**Speaker:** Dr. Rebecca Millsop — philosopher  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** Second of two philosophy of art sessions with Dr. Millsop. The first session addressed definitional questions — "what counts as art?" — drawing on analytic and conceptual philosophy. This session turns to functional and political frameworks: not *what* art is, but *what art does* and *why it matters*. Four major continental philosophers are covered in sequence: Walter Benjamin, Martin Heidegger, Theodor Adorno, and Jacques Rancière. Students work with timed reflection exercises throughout, applying each framework to their own practice. Companion Toolkit PDF extends the frameworks with further examples and questions.

---

## Overview

Dr. Millsop presents four frameworks not as competing definitions to adjudicate between, but as **tools in a toolkit** — each illuminating different aspects of what art does in the world. Together they form a progression: from the question of *presence and reproducibility* (Benjamin) to *disclosure of truth* (Heidegger) to *autonomous resistance* (Adorno) to *redistribution of the sensible* (Rancière). By the end of the session, students have both the definitional vocabulary from Part 1 and the functional/political vocabulary to answer the harder question: not just "is this art?" but "why does my work matter, and what does it do?"

## 1. Art as Reproducible Object — Walter Benjamin (1935)

### The Concept of Aura

Benjamin's landmark essay "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" introduces **aura**: the unique presence, authenticity, and ritual quality of an artwork existing in a particular time and place. Aura is the sense of encountering an original — something irreducible to its copies, embedded in history and tradition.

**The historical transition:**
- Pre-mechanical reproduction: art was aura-laden and ritualistic — attached to religious ceremony, tied to specific places, rare in its experience
- Post-mechanical reproduction (photography, film): infinite copies become possible; aura is destroyed or withered; art escapes temples and enters the street

**Benjamin's argument on what this enables:** Mechanical reproduction is not simply loss. It liberates art from its ritual function and makes possible a new political function. Art can now address mass publics, circulate freely, and be experienced outside institutions of exclusion.

**Two divergent paths forward:**
- **Art for art's sake** — artificially recreating aura in reproduced form = reactionary; nostalgic attempt to restore what reproducibility destroyed
- **Politicized art** — embracing and harnessing reproduction = progressive; using distribution as a political tool

**Benjamin's own ambivalence:** He mourns the loss of aura even while celebrating its destruction. This tension is productive rather than a contradiction to resolve.

### Contemporary Case Studies

**Rothko Chapel (Houston):** Has aura — specific presence, specific place, transformative encounter — yet is free, public, and non-denominational. Democratic aura.

**Mona Lisa:** Most reproduced image in history, yet millions pilgrimage to the Louvre to see the small original. The original retains aura despite — perhaps because of — its infinite reproduction.

**Kusama's Infinity Rooms:** Made of mirrors; reproduction *is* the medium. Yet they generate hour-long queues. The experience of the room is irreproducible even when the mechanism is replication.

**Sherry Levine (Pictures Generation):** Re-photographed Walker Evans's Depression-era photographs and exhibited the results as her own work. Makes the tension of reproduction visible and material — the act of appropriation is the critical statement.

**Trevor Paglen:** Pulls invisible surveillance imagery into visibility — photographs surveillance satellites, facial recognition datasets, images made by machines for machines with no intended human viewer. Explores what happens when images exist outside human perception.

### The Studio Question

The tension between accessibility/circulation and presence/aura is real for working artists. You don't have to choose a side — but naming which side you're on in a given work is a critical decision. Does the work gain or lose meaning when widely reproduced? Is that gain or loss the point?

## 2. Art as Truth Event — Martin Heidegger (1930s)

### The Central Claim

For Heidegger, art doesn't *represent* truth — art is where truth *happens*. His key term is the Greek *aletheia*: truth as unconcealment, the bringing-forth of what was hidden.

**The temple example:** A Greek temple doesn't imitate or depict anything. But it "gathers a world" around it — makes rocks appear as rock, sky as sky, makes the sea visible as sea. It creates an opening where hidden things become unhidden. It doesn't represent the landscape; it discloses it.

**World vs. Earth:** Every significant artwork holds in tension two forces:
- **World** — the meaningful context, the human social order the work makes visible
- **Earth** — the material resistance, the opacity of matter, what refuses to be fully explained or used

Great art holds this tension; it neither explains everything nor remains mute.

**Alvin Noë's contemporary translation (UC Berkeley philosopher):** Art is a "strange tool" — it takes organized activities we do automatically (seeing, moving, perceiving) and puts them *on display*, making the invisible structure of habitual experience visible. Art defamiliarizes the habitual.

**Important caveat:** Heidegger was a Nazi party member. His philosophy's relationship to his politics remains contested and unresolved. The framework is used here with that biographical and historical context in full view.

### Contemporary Examples

**James Turrell — Sky Spaces:** Viewers look up through an aperture in a ceiling. Turrell's work with light and perception creates an experience where the sky appears to detach from depth and become a surface — almost material, almost touchable. The work discloses something normally invisible about how we see sky.

**Anika Yee:** Installations involving biological processes — bacteria cultures, tempura-fried flowers, kelp. Makes invisible ecological and biological life perceptible; the scale and process of what we share the world with, normally below our threshold of attention, becomes the encounter.

**Conceptual challenges to Heidegger:** Anne Truitt's date paintings, John Baldessari's "I will not make any more boring art," Martin Creed's lights on/off — these are harder to fit into a disclosure framework. They don't obviously "gather a world" or unconceal in the traditional sense. The framework has limits.

### The Studio Question

What organized activity does your work put on display? What does it make visible about perception, embodiment, movement, or social experience that normally goes unnoticed? If your answer is unclear, that's useful information — it means the work is doing something different from disclosure.

## 3. Art as Autonomous Practice — Theodor Adorno (post-1970)

### The Paradox

Adorno's core thesis: **art serves society best by *not* serving it.** The work that most resists being absorbed, used, or consumed is the most socially important.

**The mechanism:** Under capitalism, everything becomes commodified — turned into product, made predictable, made consumable. The culture industry produces entertainment calibrated to existing desires, never challenging them. Real art — art that refuses to please, that is deliberately difficult — is the one thing that resists this absorption.

**The political claim:** Difficulty and refusal are not aesthetic positions only. They are political resistance, more powerful than explicit political messaging, because they operate at the level of form and experience rather than content.

### The Controversial Example: Schoenberg vs. Jazz

For Adorno, Schoenberg's 12-tone atonal music represents the model: maximally difficult, maximally resistant to entertainment and commerce. Jazz, by contrast, he argued, fit the culture industry — was absorbed, commercialized, made predictable.

**Adorno was profoundly wrong about jazz, and his error was rooted in racism.** His framework couldn't recognize innovation and resistance outside the European classical tradition. This failure is not a footnote — it reveals a structural limit of the framework: it privileges certain traditions by default.

The broader insight still holds (capitalism absorbs resistance and makes it product) while the specific application was deeply flawed. Use the framework's insight while holding its Eurocentrism in full view.

### Two Contrasting Images

**Shepard Fairey's *Hope* poster:** Explicitly political, widely circulated, immediately successful. Also immediately absorbed — printed on T-shirts and mugs within weeks of its 2008 appearance. For Adorno, commercial success of political art signals its absorption, not its efficacy.

**Agnes Martin's grid paintings:** Quiet, repetitive, non-signaling, deeply resistant to spectacle and quick consumption. Harder to market, harder to explain, harder to appropriate. For Adorno, *more* politically powerful precisely because it refuses ease.

### Claire Bishop's Extension

Bishop applies Adorno's logic to participatory and social practice art. She distinguishes:
- **Antagonistic social practice** (genuine friction, creates dissensus): Santiago Sierra's work — paid unemployed men to be tattooed in a gallery; makes exploitation visible rather than resolving it
- **Instrumental community art** (feel-good, resolves tension): work that does things in the world (community renovation, cultural center creation) but aestheticizes social problems rather than exposing them

Theaster Gates's community-engaged work falls in the second category for Bishop, which doesn't make it less valuable as civic work — only differently valued by this framework.

### Counter-examples to Adorno

The framework cannot account for work that is both accessible and serious:

- **Carrie Mae Weems — Kitchen Table Series:** Immediately readable, emotionally direct, widely accessible, deeply important. Not difficult in Adorno's sense, but not absorbed in the culture industry's sense either.
- **Yinka Shonibare:** Post-colonial critique through Victorian mannequins wearing African wax-print fabric — accessible, commercially successful, and genuinely critical simultaneously
- **Do Ho Suh:** Fabric replicas of childhood homes and apartments — visually stunning, commercially successful, serious work on displacement, migration, and memory

These works suggest the framework is one tool, not the only measure.

### The Studio Question

Do you see accessibility as compromise or commitment? Both positions are philosophical. Adorno gives permission to refuse palatability when the work demands it — but the framework also has real limits in what it can recognize as politically serious. What is your practice's relationship to difficulty and accessibility?

## 4. Art as Redistribution of the Sensible — Jacques Rancière (contemporary)

### The Central Concept

For Rancière, art is whatever **redistributes the sensible** — reconfigures what is visible, audible, sayable, and thinkable in a given social order.

**The sensible** (le partage du sensible): A configuration of what counts as meaningful perception versus noise; who is seen as a speaking subject versus background; what is worthy of attention. This is not fixed — it is a historically contingent arrangement of what can be perceived as mattering.

**Art's political operation:** Not through message or content but through formal operations. Art challenges existing distributions of who gets to speak, be seen, and participate — by making the previously invisible visible, the previously inaudible audible, the previously unsayable sayable.

**The difference from Heidegger:** Heidegger asks what a painting *reveals* about the being of things — Van Gogh's peasant shoes reveal the world of the peasant. Rancière's move is different: the political disruption is that a peasant's shoes are *worthy of being painted at all*. That choice of subject matter is itself the redistribution.

### The Aesthetic Regime

Rancière locates the historical beginning of the "aesthetic regime" roughly in the eighteenth century — defined by a specific experience that breaks down hierarchies between high and low subjects, worthy and unworthy topics, elevated and common forms. The aesthetic regime makes it possible for a peasant's shoe or a trash heap to be treated as the subject of serious art.

**Not formalism:** Form is the mechanism, not the end. The politics is in what formal choices *do* to perception and visibility — not in form for its own sake.

**The risk:** Too permissive — if art is whatever redistributes the sensible, what *doesn't* qualify? The framework can make almost anything sound politically serious. Needs to be used carefully.

### Contemporary Examples

**Kerry James Marshall:** Large-scale paintings of Black domestic and social life, using intensely saturated Black pigment for figures. The monumental treatment — scale, craft, ambition — challenges art historical hierarchies about whose life deserves epic painterly attention. The redistribution is in the decision to paint this way, at this scale, in this tradition.

**Torquay C. Dyson:** Geometric abstractions exploring the spatial politics of race, architecture, and movement. Makes visible whose freedom of movement through space is constrained, whose presence in certain spaces is treated as anomalous — not through depiction but through formal choices about geometry and space.

**Forensic Architecture:** Reconstructs invisible spaces — secret prisons, sites of building disasters — through evidence and testimony. Makes corporate and state violence perceptible through architectural analysis. What was deliberately kept unseen becomes legible through their method.

### The Studio Question

What does your work make visible, audible, or sayable that wasn't before? What existing distribution of attention, value, or subjectivity are you challenging? If the answer is unclear, it may mean the work is reinforcing rather than redistributing.

## The Four Frameworks in Relation

| Framework | Key Question | Political Mechanism | Limit |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------|-|
| **Benjamin** | Does the work have presence or only circulation? | Reproduction as liberation *or* loss of ritual power | Ambivalent — doesn't fully resolve |
| **Heidegger** | What does the work unconceal? | Art as disclosure of what habitual perception hides | Conceptual and political art are harder to fit; Nazi affiliation unresolved |
| **Adorno** | Does the work resist being absorbed? | Difficulty and autonomy as political resistance | Eurocentrism; can't recognize accessible work as serious |
| **Rancière** | What does the work redistribute? | Formal operations that reconfigure who/what is visible, audible, sayable | Risk of over-application; almost anything can be framed this way |

No single framework is correct. Each illuminates something different about what a work does and what is at stake in it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **"What is art?" and "what does art do?" are different questions.** This session addresses the second — and the second is harder to duck as a working artist
- **Aura and reproduction are still the right frame for thinking about presence, distribution, and originality.** Benjamin's tension has not been resolved — it has intensified
- **Art can be where truth happens rather than where truth is represented.** Heidegger's framework asks what your work makes visible that was previously hidden — from perception, attention, or experience
- **Difficulty is a position, not a failure.** Adorno's framework gives permission to refuse palatability — but must be used without his framework's racism intact
- **Form is political without requiring political content.** Rancière's redistribution happens through formal choices — what you decide to paint, at what scale, in what tradition, in what space
- **Use these as tools, not as verdicts.** No framework can account for everything. Knowing which frameworks illuminate your work — and which don't — is itself part of knowing what you're making

## Reflection Questions

1. **Aura and your practice:** Does your work have aura — a dimension that depends on specific presence, encounter, and place? If you reproduce or circulate it widely, what happens to that dimension? Is that loss a problem, a feature, or the point?

2. **Unconcealment:** What does your work make visible that normally goes unnoticed — about perception, embodiment, social experience, or material reality? If you're unsure, what would have to change for the work to unconceal something?

3. **Resistance and absorption:** Is there a commercial or institutional version of your work that would be more palatable but less yourself? What would you have to give up to make that version? Is refusing to make it a philosophical position or just a preference?

4. **The redistribution test:** What was previously unseen, unheard, or unsayable that your work makes available — not through content but through formal choices (scale, material, subject, context, venue)? What existing hierarchy of attention does it challenge?

5. **Which framework fits?** Of the four — Benjamin, Heidegger, Adorno, Rancière — which one most accurately describes what your current work is actually doing? Which one describes what you *want* it to be doing? Is there a gap between those two answers?
