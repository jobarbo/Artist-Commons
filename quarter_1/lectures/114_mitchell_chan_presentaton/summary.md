# Mitchell F. Chan: Body of Work Presentation

**Speaker:** Mitchell F. Chan — conceptual artist (Toronto/international)  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** First of three Artist Commons sessions with Mitchell Chan. Students played his video games Xantar and Lady Boss in a prior close-looking session. This presentation covers his full arc: architecture → public art → gallery work → blockchain → video games.

---

## Overview

Mitchell Chan is one of the few contemporary artists whose career pivots cleanly into two chapters — pre- and post-NFT — yet insists those chapters are deeply continuous. The talk is a study in practical wisdom, demystification, and the recurring theme of **getting over yourself**. He traces 20+ years of building a practice through commercial work, public art, loss-leader gallery relationships, and eventually conceptual blockchain art that landed in the Venice Biennale. Almost none of it went the way he planned.

---

## 1. Getting Started: Geography, School, and the Grit Phase

**Born in Pembroke, Ontario** — five hours north of Toronto. He had to drive two hours to Ottawa to see a museum. Canada doesn't produce many art stars and doesn't sustain artist careers the way international scenes do. This fact shaped everything.

**Architecture as the responsible choice:** Chose undergraduate architecture at Carleton University rather than fine art. Looking back: "huge mistake." Architecture is "barely a job and barely artistic." But the practical skills in design, real-world constraints, iteration, and client management proved invaluable.

**The \$555 education at Interaccess:** An artist-run center in Toronto offering summer workshops. For a single payment, he gained introductions to electronics design, Processing, Max/MSP, microcontrollers, robotics, and kinetics. This "cheapest education" led to a portfolio, which led to a full scholarship to the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC) — a roughly \$90,000 value.

> "Many of us were once dumbasses. And some of us admit to being dumbasses still. The only requirement is curiosity and fearlessness to fail."

**Art school's real value is community.** The MFA is worth it not primarily for what you learn but for the network you build. After graduating from SAIC, he moved back to Canada immediately — severing that network and learning the lesson the hard way.

## 2. Public Art as a Sustainable Entry Point

Public art projects work like a normal job: open calls, defined budgets, transparent selection processes. Unlike the nebulous gallery selection world, you can succeed or fail largely on merit.

> "You are a drywall supplier. Your drywall is just formed into these vaguely artistic forms."

**UN Peacekeeping Monument (Peterborough, Ontario):** A \$75,000 CAD commission for twelve veterans. Design included concrete slabs, powder-coated aluminum, and mirror-polished stainless steel (material changed mid-project due to budget). The stainless created an "infinite hall of mirrors" effect with the robin's egg blue of UN Peacekeeper helmets. The work was profound to the veterans. It was irrelevant to city councilors (worried about budget overruns) and maintenance staff (concerned about snow plowing interference and blinding drivers).

**Lesson:** Getting over yourself is essential. Every major stakeholder in public art has different needs, none of them aesthetic.

Ten to twelve years of commercial and public projects built his skills in iteration, deadline management, and creating under pressure rather than under inspiration.

## 3. The First Really Good Artwork

**"Something National Conversation" (2017):** After moving to Toronto to pursue art seriously, it took ten years to make what he considers his first genuinely good work.

The installation featured two false walls with holes at regular intervals through which clouds of water vapor emerged and smashed into each other, leaving nothing. Chairs and People magazines were placed in the space. A generative audio system read the New York Times API in real time, chopping headlines into a stuttering collage. A pseudo-random string quartet played in the background. Made in early 2017 before the inauguration — the word "Kellyanne Conway" repeated obsessively. The work was about **insubstantial antagonism**: spectacle of conflict that produces nothing.

**The path to this piece:** At SAIC, he built cloud cannons as artistic medium — actuators and bearings generating pressurized water vapor. His thesis project programmed the machine to write out the entirety of *Don Quixote* letter by letter ("the world's stupidest dot matrix display"). It was technically ambitious and conceptually almost good.

> "Building a clever machine doesn't make you an artist. A clever machine is not an artwork."

The thesis proved he deserved a good grade. "National Conversation" was actually good because it hid the technical complexity and let the idea breathe.

> "The point of an artwork is to let somebody feel something or understand something for themselves."

## 4. The Gallery Relationship as Loss Leader

At Angel Gallery (Toronto), his experimental tech work was essentially unsellable. Gallerist Jamie Angel used one challenging piece per year to signal cutting-edge credibility — while selling profitable oil paintings for eleven months prior.

> "I'm a loss leader. I'm a door crasher at art Walmart."

Chan knew the score and accepted it. The cadence of work, exhibition, and feedback was what he needed — not necessarily the sales. This clarity about the function of a gallery relationship is part of what kept him going.

---

## 5. Digital Zones: Conceptual Blockchain Art

**The conceptual frame — Yves Klein's "Zones of Immaterial Pictorial Sensibility" (1958):** Klein sold invisible artworks — certificates for "feelings" and "sensibility." His manifesto: the canvas is an imperfect intermediary; the ideal artwork transmits sensation directly from artist to viewer. He was simultaneously deeply spiritual and fully pragmatic about the market. His receipts for immaterial pieces are both high art and financial instruments.

> "He understood so well the practicalities and vulgarities of the real world."

**Chan's insight:** Bitcoin is simply a precise measurement of all belief in Bitcoin that exists at a given moment. It is Klein's immaterial artwork made mainstream.

**"Digital Zones" (2017):** He created a smart contract on Ethereum implementing Klein's zones model. Wrote a 33-page essay called "The Blue Paper" documenting the history: Klein's immaterial artworks → Bitcoin → Ethereum → their connections. The smart contract was almost incidental to the essay. He created what amounted to a 101-edition NFT-like conceptual series before NFTs became a widespread phenomenon.

**CryptoPunks timing:** Between finishing and unveiling, CryptoPunks released. He was furious. "Getting over yourself" again.

**Reception:** Nobody cared at the initial Interaccess lecture. Eventually the work ended up in Venice — exhibited alongside original Yves Klein pieces and Marcel Duchamp's Monte Carlo bonds — and in Singapore and Berlin.

**Why it worked conceptually:** His approach was clear, rigorous, scientific, and dry with occasional humor. The Blue Paper leaves readers free to decide if crypto is a scam, just as they can decide Klein's work is a scam. Applied the same rigor to both.

**On conceptual aesthetics:**
> "Conceptual artists are underrated aestheticists. Aesthetics are still critically important — minimal aesthetic choices are stylistic choices that teach the audience how to engage."

## 6. Video Games as Medium

**The wrong reasons to make game art:** Most artists enter wanting to be god-like shapers of worlds, experiencing "true creation" through real-time 3D environments. This motivation doesn't lead to good artwork.

**The counter-example — Bill Viola:** Arguably the world's most celebrated video artist. Made a game available on PlayStation with USC Game Lab. It failed as art because a video artist's job is to present a point of view compositionally — deciding camera placement and frame contents. Relinquishing that control means no composition.

> "You've just created a bunch of conditions that the viewer can fuck up."

**The actual medium:** What's interesting is not doing more (the Gesamtkunstwerk impulse is usually wrong). What's interesting is **a person's ability to mess up your art**. User agency and player autonomy become the medium.

**Design philosophy in Xantar and Lady Boss:** Every element reinforces what the player thinks they control versus what they don't. Different graphic styles contrast controllable and uncontrollable elements. Cinematography supports this phenomenon. All art forms in the game point toward one thing.

---

## 7. Simplification and the Pinball Machines

**Rumspringa phase:** Recently paused video game work to take time between projects. Made physical artworks — specifically pinball machines. "How can I make a physical video game?"

Fully interactive machines where players hit flippers — but the flippers have zero impact on the bumper score. The game continues regardless of player presence. The amount of interactivity is irrelevant.

**The distillation principle:** Latest work is a sculptural version of Lady Boss — game continuing without player. An exercise in simplifying and distilling. A reversal of the Gesamtkunstwerk impulse.

> "Physical artwork is fun. It's fun to make things and fail at it and do stuff that AI can't do."

---

## 8. On AI and the Noise Problem

**Why he stopped doing blockchain conceptual art:** When nobody cared about crypto, his text became a guiding light for both people in crypto trying to understand art and curators trying to understand crypto without seeming capitalist. Once the discourse exploded, the gap was filled.

> "If I had something really smart to say about AI, I got bad news. It's kind of too late. It's such noise out there."

**On "being very online":** Social media creates an uncanny mirror — a feedback loop around a curated slice of yourself. The noise of what's dropping today has very little to do with what makes a sensation-based encounter worth having.

> "The artists are creating sensations. Scientists create functions, philosophers create concepts, artists create sensations. What sensorial aggregate are you bringing in response to what you are seeing?"

## Key Takeaways

- **Getting over yourself is the foundational skill.** It shows up at every stage: architecture school, art school, public art, commercial work, gallery relationships, digital art success
- **Public art is the most meritocratic part of the art world.** Open calls, defined budgets, transparent processes — you can win or lose on mostly merit
- **Commercial skills build fine art capacity.** Managing projects, budgets, and clients under pressure all informed the gallery practice
- **Clever machines are not artworks.** Technical achievement is not the same as art; the work needs to let viewers feel or understand something for themselves
- **The loss leader model can be the right model.** Accepting your gallery role honestly is better than chasing a position you don't have yet
- **User agency is the medium in game-based art.** What players do with their freedom — that is the interesting thing
- **Simplify, distill, reduce.** The direction of his most recent work is always toward essence, not accumulation
- **Noise is real.** When discourse explodes around a technology, the window for conceptual clarity often closes

---

## Reflection Questions

1. **Getting over yourself:** Where in your own practice are you holding onto an idea of what you're doing that doesn't match what your work actually requires? What would it look like to let that go?

2. **Technical achievement vs. art:** Is there a clever system or method in your current work that you're leading with? If you simplified it to make the idea visible, what would you lose?

3. **The loss leader question:** What gallery, institution, or opportunity are you pursuing right now? Is your work a fit for their actual program, or are you imagining a relationship that doesn't match reality?

4. **User agency:** If you work with interactive systems, what does a viewer/user do with the agency you give them? Is that activity the interesting part of the work?

5. **The noise test:** Is there something you want to say about a current technology or cultural moment? Is the discourse around it already so saturated that your contribution would be signal or noise?
