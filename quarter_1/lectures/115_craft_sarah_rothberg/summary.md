# Craft Session: Affordances, Constraints, and Ecosystem Thinking

**Speaker:** Sarah Rothberg — artist, professor at NYU ITP (Interactive Telecommunications Program)  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** Second Artist Commons craft session. Sarah works in interactive media, performance, installation, and workshops. Her practice sits at the intersection of teaching and art-making; she recently had a solo show at Bitforms Gallery. The session is structured around five interactive activities designed to help artists break creative ruts and reconceptualize their practice through a single conceptual lens: affordances.

---

## Overview

This session is a working toolkit, not a lecture. Sarah moves through five activities in sequence, each building on the last — from brainstorming the hidden possibilities in an everyday object, to applying them to your actual artistic practice, to prototyping the smallest possible version of something new. The unifying idea is **ecosystem thinking**: every element of your practice — medium, context, audience, form, platform — is negotiable, and examining it through the lens of affordances reveals possibilities you couldn't see before.

---

## 1. What Are Affordances?

**The term's origin:** James J. Gibson coined "affordance" in *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979). He needed a word that referred simultaneously to the environment and the animal within it:

> "The affordances of the environment are what it offers to the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill."

**UX design adaptation:** Donald Norman popularized the concept in *The Design of Everyday Things* (1980s). In design: plates are for pushing, knobs are for turning, slots are for inserting. A doorknob shaped like a hand affords turning; a flat panel affords pushing. A confusing door has a pull-shaped handle on a push door — the affordance misleads.

**In art:** Affordances aren't just about usability. When applied to artistic practice, they reveal:
- What a medium, tool, material, or context *offers* — obvious uses and hidden ones
- What an artwork *does* to its audience (not just what it represents)
- What a platform or venue *affords* in terms of reach, interaction, or meaning

**Hidden affordances:** Musicians use "extended techniques" — plucking piano strings, using the body of a cello as percussion. This reveals affordances the instrument wasn't designed for. Every medium has analogous hidden possibilities.

---

## 2. Art and Its Publics

**Michael Warner's framework** ("Publics and Counterpublics"): Art does one of two things — it speaks to an *existing* public (provides solidarity, acts as an example for an audience already formed) or it creates a *counterpublic* (a temporary public brought into existence by the work itself).

Either way, when art exists in the world, it exists within an **ecosystem**. Understanding that ecosystem — and the affordances of each part of it — gives artists a way to zoom out and see the full set of choices available to them: author, context, audience, form, venue, platform.

**Example — Jenny Holzer:**
> "I like placing content where people look and that can be at the bottom of a cup or a shirt or a hat. I use language because I want to offer content that people — not necessarily art people — could understand."

Holzer understands the affordances of medium, tool, context, and material simultaneously.

## 3. The Five Activities

### Activity 1: Object Affordances Brainstorm (3 minutes)

Pick any object from your immediate environment. List every possible affordance — obvious and absurd — without filtering. Exhaust the possibilities.

*Example from the session (tape measure):*
- Obvious: measuring waist, height, liquids
- Unusual: jump rope, pull-up bar, hotel door security measure, chopstick holder
- Revealing: a vinyl tape measure stretches when hung — it expresses *something* through its physical properties

**The point:** The exercise opens up the full range of what a thing offers before you narrow in on any one use. It trains the habit of not defaulting to the first affordance.

---

### Activity 2: Conveying Concept Through a Single Affordance

Pick one affordance from your list. Choose one concept from a provided list (confused but hiding it, loneliness, joy despite circumstances, etc.). Use only that affordance to convey that concept — as performance, sculpture, documented action, or any form.

**Why this works:** Constraining yourself to one affordance forces creative specificity. It also surfaces what a material or object *does* that representation alone cannot.

---

### Activity 3: Random Material + Personal Practice

**Step 1:** Think of a number 1–10, use it to randomly select a material or technology from a list (not your usual medium).

**Step 2:** List the affordances of that random material.

**Step 3:** Pick one affordance and connect it to something from your *actual* practice — a theme, metaphor, image, or question you genuinely care about.

This is **A-to-C thinking**: start from something known (your practice), bring in something random (the material), and let the friction between them reveal artistic inclinations and ways of working you didn't know were there.

*Example from the session:* A participant thought about mycelium and its affordance of "growing in patterns." Idea: set up a Petri dish with marked areas, let mycelium grow, use which areas it covers to generate numbers — an organic random number generator. Sarah connected this to how a kid in Minecraft used a cow stepping on a 3×3 grid of pressure plates as a random number generator, and how a cryptographic company uses lava lamps for true randomness. The affordance unlocked a genuine research direction.

**The randomness is the point.** Loosening your grip on your own tendencies — through the constraint of randomness — keeps you fresh and honest. It prevents making only work you already know how to make.

---

### Activity 4: Wants / Don't Wants / Okay Withs

Free-write for 2–3 minutes about what's exciting about your current work or project. Then sort your thoughts into three columns:

| I want | I don't want | I'm okay with |
|--------|-------------|---------------|
| It to be clear | It to be too long | It being kind of ugly |

The **"okay with"** column is the crucial one. It captures what you'll accept — the flexible middle ground that often reveals unstated assumptions about the work.

Optional advanced step: for each "want," describe what full fulfillment, partial fulfillment, and no fulfillment looks like. This sharpens whether you've actually achieved your goals without trapping you in something paralyzing.

---

### Activity 5: Two-Axis Decision Matrix (Partner Exercise)

Pair up. Each person explains their current project (referencing the wants/don'ts/okays list if comfortable). The listener identifies **opposing tensions** in what they hear — pairs of competing desires:

- Broad audience vs. expert audience
- Small scale vs. large scale
- Inside vs. outside
- Intimate vs. public
- Made of wood vs. made of metal
- For kids vs. for adults

The listener draws a two-axis matrix — one tension per axis. The axes don't need to be related to each other.

**Then:** Imagine a version of your project in each of the four quadrants. What does it look like? What are its constraints? Which version has the most from your "wants" list, the fewest from "don'ts," and a manageable number from "okays"?

**For process-based / emergent artists:** The matrix doesn't have to apply to the work itself. It can apply to exhibition decisions — how to hang it, which pieces to show, how to frame the experience.

**The value:** You're not being told what to make. You're generating a map of possibilities that you then prototype.

## 4. Prototyping as Method

Sarah draws a distinction between the **waterfall approach** (dream up the finished thing, then build it all the way down) and the **agile / iterative approach** (build the smallest functional version first, test it, then grow).

> "Can I first make a skateboard?"

The skateboard answers one question: do people want to move faster using wheels? If yes, add a handle → scooter. Then → bicycle → motorcycle → car. Each iteration tests one assumption before committing to the next.

For visual artists: a sketch that still carries the emotional quality of the finished work. For installation: a model that answers one spatial question. The prototype should feel complete at its scale — not like a rough draft, but like a small, honest version of the real thing.

**Why this matters:** Rather than spending six months toward something new, failing, and feeling devastated — prototype the smallest version, let it be generative, discover what it makes possible.

## 5. The Bigger Picture: Inventing the Camera

In 2016, Sarah co-taught a VR course with filmmaker Carol Dysinger. Dysinger said to filmmakers: "When you're making a movie, it's probably going to end up in a movie theater or on Netflix."

To the artists, she said something different:

> "For a lot of artists, one of the possibilities you have is to invent the movie theater."

And further still:

> "As an artist, you might be inventing the camera."

Everything is at play — the artifact, where it shows, the form, how it's consumed, how it's made. Most people assume the platform is fixed and work within it. Artists have the option to question the platform itself.

**This is what ecosystem thinking enables.** When you understand the affordances of every layer — material, form, venue, audience, technology, context — you can choose which layers to accept and which to redesign.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Affordances reveal what things offer, not just what they're for.** Every medium, tool, context, and platform has hidden affordances worth excavating
- **Randomness is a creative tool.** Loosening your grip on your habitual ways of working — through random constraints — prevents you from only making work you already know how to make
- **The "okay with" column matters as much as the "want" column.** Articulating what you'll tolerate clarifies what you actually need
- **The decision matrix generates possibilities, not answers.** You're mapping the space; you still choose where to go
- **Prototype before committing.** Build the skateboard first — test one assumption before building the car
- **Artists can invent the camera.** Platform, form, context, and audience are all negotiable — not just the work itself
- **Ecosystem thinking makes all of this visible.** Your practice is a system of relations; understanding each part's affordances gives you leverage on the whole

## Reflection Questions

1. **The object exercise, applied:** Pick a material or medium central to your current practice. List every affordance — obvious and absurd — without filtering. What shows up in the unusual ones that you haven't used yet?

2. **Hidden affordances:** What "extended technique" exists for your primary medium — a use it wasn't designed for that reveals something new about it?

3. **The random material:** Apply Activity 3 to your practice right now. Pick a random number 1–10, select a material from a list, find its affordances, and connect one of them to something you actually care about. What does the friction between them reveal?

4. **Wants / don't wants / okay withs:** Free-write about your current project, then sort the thoughts into the three columns. What surprises you in the "okay with" column?

5. **The camera question:** What platform, venue, or context are you assuming is fixed in your current practice? What would it mean to question that assumption — to invent the movie theater, or the camera?
