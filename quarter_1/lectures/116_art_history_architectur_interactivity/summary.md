# Art History: Architectures of Interactivity

**Speaker:** Polina Rud — art historian  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** First of two art history lectures on interactivity, timed after students played Mitchell Chan's video games in a close-looking session. The lecture traces how art shifted from static objects to dynamic systems — from the Wagnerian stage to cybernetic installations — and asks what it means for a viewer to be "activated" by a work. Companion lecture: 119 (Narrative Interactivity).

---

## Overview

This lecture covers roughly a century of art history through a single lens: the evolving relationship between artwork and viewer. It moves in three waves — from **field** (art as dynamic environment you enter), to **cybernetics** (art as feedback system you're inside), to **the black box** (art as system whose internal logic stays hidden). Each wave raises the same question with increasing complexity: when a viewer acts, how free are they really?

---

## 1. From Static Object to Dynamic Field (1870s–1960s)

### Wagner and the Gesamtkunstwerk (1876)

Richard Wagner's concept of the *Gesamtkunstwerk* ("total work of art") proposed a fusion of music, poetry, architecture, and performance into a single organic whole. His Festspielhaus in Bayreuth (1876) — darkened hall, hidden orchestra, inclined stage — was the physical embodiment: an immersive dramatic environment designed to produce collective catharsis.

**Limitation:** The viewer remained passive. Transformation happened through empathy and affect, not action.

---

### Early Kinetic Experiments (1913–1950s)

These works begin to move the viewer from observer to variable:

- **Marcel Duchamp — Bicycle Wheel (1913):** Object requiring viewer activation; the work exists as potential until set in motion
- **Léon Theremin — Theremin (1920):** Played without physical contact; the performer moves through invisible electromagnetic fields; space becomes interface; spectator can become musician
- **László Moholy-Nagy — Light Space Modulator:** Rotating light machine transforming space into dynamic field; viewer position changes the image
- **Naum Gabo — Kinetic Construction (1919–20):** Single metal rod on a motor; vibration creates visual illusion of a continuous wave
- **Alexander Calder — Mobiles (1932–76):** Suspended elements responding to air currents; self-adjusting system where boundaries between work, environment, and spectator become fluid

**Calder and politics:** Created during the rise of fascism, Calder's mobiles challenge monumental fascist aesthetics through lightness and anti-hierarchy — not by depicting politics, but through form.

> "He challenges it through form. I'm not depicting reality, I'm copying reality. I'm creating some form that tests it, that interrogates it, that challenges it."

**Key shift:** Art moves from **static object to dynamic field**. The viewer moves from **external observer to active component**. The work is no longer fully determined by the artist.

---

### Op Art and Perceptual Participation (1950s–60s)

Interactivity no longer requires motors or physical manipulation — it operates at the level of the retina and brain.

- **Bridget Riley:** Repeated patterns of lines and curves producing dizziness, pulsation, shifting depth. Studied Seurat's pointillism to understand how small units create powerful perceptual vibrations
- **Giulio Alviani:** Polished aluminum surfaces where even small spectator movements fracture light dramatically. Moving a spotlight centimeters transforms the entire visual effect
- **Jesús Rafael Soto — Suspended Thread Installations:** Viewer body enters the work physically; suspended elements create porous space the participant can traverse. Interactivity operates simultaneously on retinal and corporeal levels — vision becomes tactile
- **Victor Vasarely:** Imagined visual systems extending beyond canvas into architecture and urban space. Conceptualized an "Op Art City" where visual instability becomes urban ideology — perception training as a form of active subjectivity

**Key theorist — Maurice Merleau-Ponty:** Perception doesn't emerge from abstract consciousness but from embodied experience. "We are body" — not simply "we have body." In interactive art, perception becomes an action of the body within environment. Like Heraclitus: you never step into the same river twice; you never step into the same artwork twice.

**Key theorist — Frank Popper:** Involving the viewer in an artwork is a step toward democratic society. If individuals learn to perceive actively, they cease to be passive consumers. **Activation is not merely physical movement — it is a form of training subjectivity.**

---

### Lygia Clark and Hélio Oiticica (1950s–70s)

**Lygia Clark — "The Houses, the Body" and "Nostalgia of the Body":**
- Participants physically move through components (tunnels, membranes, chambers) producing sensations of instability, enclosure, transformation
- Objects (hoods, masks, goggles, gloves) stimulate touch, weight, pressure — deliberately destabilizing vision to shift perception toward bodily awareness
- Art resembles a sensory and therapeutic experiment more than a conventional artwork

**Hélio Oiticica — Parangolés (1964–68):**
- Colorful capes of fabric and plastic banners meant to be worn, not viewed
- Developed after visits to the Mangueira favela in Rio de Janeiro; collaborated with the Mangueira Samba School
- Dissolved distance between museum art and marginalized social realities
- **Participation becomes encounter with a different social space and rhythm of life.** Activating the viewer becomes a gesture of solidarity and social empathy

**Late-1960s shift:** Interactivity becomes a methodology for *studying* what happens when the viewer is activated — not just activating them. Enzo Mari's **Modulo 856** included an Umberto Eco-designed questionnaire to stimulate reflection through questions. Meaning begins to be researched and analyzed.

## 2. Cybernetics, Feedback, and the Black Box (1948–1980s)

### The Founding Moment: Norbert Wiener (1948)

Wiener's *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* introduced **feedback** as a governing principle: control no longer exercised from a single center but distributed throughout a system. The system maintains dynamic balance through continuous signal exchange — constantly correcting and recalibrating.

**Critical tension:** The system listens and responds. But control is embedded directly inside communication. Every response functions as behavioral correction. Signals regulate, normalize deviations, maintain equilibrium. The apparent dialogue is also a mechanism of control.

---

### Early Cybernetic Artworks

**1953 — Cordon-Poses Music Collar:**  
Device responding to musical performance by generating color and light patterns. If the musician repeated the same melody too long, the apparatus would "lose interest" and stop responding. **Boredom itself functioned as a signal.** The machine dictated the rhythm of interaction; the musician became the adaptive agent.

**1956 — Nicolas Schoeffer Cybernetic Sculpture:**  
Equipped with microphones and photoreceptors, adapting behavior in response to light and sound. Schoeffer imagined environments with audiovisual programs continuously regulating human perception and mood.

**October 1966 — Nine Evenings: Theatre and Engineering (New York):**  
Event by Experiments in Art and Technology (E.A.T.), co-founded by Robert Rauschenberg. Ten artists including John Cage and Merce Cunningham collaborated with Bell Labs engineers.

- **"Open Score":** Tennis rackets fitted with radio transmitters; ball striking strings triggered sounds and gradually turned off lights. As darkness fell, cameras activated — the crowd became data, movements translated into images projected on screens. **Double-level interactivity:** surface (human-technology feedback) and deeper (collective presence absorbed as informational field)
- **John Cage & David Tudor — "Variations":** Telephone lines, microphones, radio frequencies fed into a loudspeaker network. The system generated sound only in absence of movement; activity disrupted the sonic output. **Stillness produces resonance; movement mutes the body.** Interactivity becomes negotiation with constraint

**Erwin Felsträger — Interactive Installation:**  
Large chessboard with political figures; participants moved pieces triggering transmitted signals and changing the visual/sonic environment. Playful metaphor for global political negotiation — training the socially active citizen.

---

### The Critical Concept: Heidegger's Gestell (Enframing)

Modern technology fundamentally structures how the world appears to us. Nature is no longer encountered as river or forest but as resource — hydroelectric potential, timber supply, extractable energy. Everything becomes *Bestand* (standing reserve): available for storage, calculation, optimization. **Humans too become part of the standing reserve, integrated into technological systems as functional elements.**

A smartphone appears as a communication tool. The logic of enframing turns behavior into data.

---

### Participation and Constraint

**Grey Walter's Tortoises (Elmer and Elsie, 1950s):**  
Autonomous vehicles with two-neuron circuits reacting to light, obstacles, and battery levels. Behavior appeared lifelike. But apparent autonomy was illusion — responses were fixed. Participation exists; the structure of possibilities remains rigid.

**Akira Kanayama — Paint Marker Car:**  
Remote-controlled toy car with a paint marker on canvas. The gesture-to-image translation is indirect (signal → motor → rotation → acceleration). Freedom is mechanically conditioned; there is a chain of mediation between intention and mark.

---

### "Cybernetics Serendipity" Exhibition (1968)

Landmark exhibition representing a moment of technological optimism. Two key works:

**Sam (Sound-Activated Mobile) — Oskar Schlemmer:**  
Two-meter-tall metal flower structure on a hydraulic platform, with four microphones detecting sound direction. It listened to whispers rather than loud stimuli — appeared to *choose* what to respond to. Yet an algorithm defined the accessible frequency range, intensity, and movement limits. **Participation functions as trigger; architecture defines all responses.** The participation-constraint conflict made visible and material.

**"Sensor" — Ignatovich:**  
Large hydraulic structure with microphones and motion sensors approaching or withdrawing from visitors based on stimulus intensity. Created the impression of psychological negotiation, temperament, preferences. As Jack Burnham noted: unpredictability doesn't imply freedom; spontaneity emerges from complex internal processes we cannot see.

**"Colloquy of Mobiles" (Pask):**  
Suspended mobiles: geometric/mechanical "male" units emitting light signals; organic "female" units reflecting them. If reflected light returned to the correct sensor, the system interpreted it as successful communication. Visitors could redirect light beams with mirrors. Technical interaction became a legible metaphor for social relations.

---

### Myron Krueger — Artificial Reality (1970s)

Constructed environments where participants' bodies became directly integrated into a digital feedback loop. Floor covered with sensors functioning like musical keys — each step triggered a different sound or tone. Direct communication between artist and participant disappears; the system itself becomes the mediator.

> "Interactivity promises freedom, yet behavior is designed. Activity is not the same as power. The viewer acts within a certain protocol, learns to adapt to systems in order to receive a response."

## 3. Transparency vs. the Black Box

### The Core Tension

The more intelligent a system appears, the easier it becomes to mistake programmed agency for genuine freedom. Cybernetics performs a double function:
- **Trains subjectivity** — participants learn to act within a responsive environment
- **Reveals the limits of freedom** — shows where rules structure behavior

This duality gives cybernetic art its philosophical sharpness and contemporary relevance.

**The Black Box:** A system receives signals (sound, light, movement, heat) and responds — but internal operations remain hidden. The user doesn't know what happened between input and output.

---

### Key Black Box Works

**Ivan Sutherland — "Sword of Damocles" (1968):**  
Often considered the first virtual reality experiment. Heavy head-mounted display suspended from a mechanical arm; simple 3D graphics reorganized in response to bodily movement. User saw the result but not how it happened. Sutherland's essay "The Ultimate Display" described a room where computers simulate reality convincingly enough that virtual objects would have physical consequences. **Emphasis shifts from understanding mechanisms to producing convincing behavioral effects.**

**Morton Heilig — Sensorama (1962):**  
Stereoscopic film combined with surround sound, vibrating seating, air bursts, and scents. Interactivity operates through total sensory immersion. The richer the sensory environment, the less transparent it is.

**David Rokeby — Camera-Tracking Installation:**  
Cameras detect participant movement, tracking trajectories, speed, and gestures. The mapping between gesture and sound remains invisible — why a particular gesture produces a particular tone, what thresholds determine sensitivity, what boundaries structure interaction — all unknown.

## Key Concepts

**Activation:** Transformation of viewer from passive observer to active component; requires bodily presence and decision-making. Not mere physical movement — a form of training subjectivity.

**The Black Box:** System whose internal logic remains hidden to the user. Input and output are visible; the process between them is opaque. Creates a sense of agency while obscuring control mechanisms.

**Cybernetics:** Science of feedback and systems; control distributed throughout a system rather than exercised from a single center. The system maintains dynamic balance through continuous signal exchange and behavioral correction.

**Enframing (Gestell):** The way technology fundamentally structures how reality appears to us — turning everything, including humans, into standing reserve available for calculation and optimization.

**Homeostatic Flow:** A system maintaining dynamic balance through continuous feedback; the spectator placed within this circulating loop where their action becomes part of a regulatory system.

**Interactivity as Democratic Gesture:** Movement from hierarchy toward distributed agency — yet this democracy is often constrained by hidden architectures.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The history of interactive art is a history of the viewer's expanding role** — from passive observer to active component to trapped participant
- **Activation is not the same as freedom.** Every interactive system is also an architecture of constraint — the range of possible action is always designed in advance
- **The black box is the dominant condition of contemporary interactive media.** From cybernetic sculptures to smartphones, we experience inputs and outputs while the internal logic remains opaque
- **Heidegger's enframing applies directly to interactive art.** When viewers become behavioral data, they enter the standing reserve
- **Form can be political without depicting politics.** Calder's mobiles, Clark's capes, Oiticica's parangolés challenge hierarchical aesthetics through structure, not content
- **Contemporary relevance:** From the interactive museum environments of the 1970s to today's interfaces that promise choice while delivering pre-written architectures — the question of where genuine agency lives is unresolved

---

## Reflection Questions

1. **Activation vs. freedom:** In your own interactive work (or work you want to make), what is the actual range of action available to a viewer? How much of that range did you design in advance?

2. **The black box question:** What would it look like for a viewer to understand the internal logic of your work — not just see its output? Is that opacity a feature or a problem?

3. **Enframing:** If technology structures how everything appears as standing reserve, does your work reinforce that logic or disrupt it? How?

4. **Form as politics:** Can you think of a formal choice in your practice — scale, material, arrangement, duration — that makes a political argument without depicting politics?

5. **Hidden affordances:** Thinking of your primary medium, what "extended technique" exists — a use it wasn't designed for that reveals something about it that conventional use obscures?
