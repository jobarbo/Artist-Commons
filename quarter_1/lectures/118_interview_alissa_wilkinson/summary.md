# Interview: Alissa Wilkinson — Film Criticism, Festivals, and the Business of Cinema

**Speaker:** Alissa Wilkinson — film critic, *New York Times*  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** An extended conversation with a working professional film critic, timed to Oscar season. Wilkinson is one of two full-time film critics at the NYT; previously senior critic at Vox, author of three books, and jury member at Sundance, Doc NYC, Sheffield Doc Fest, and the Hamptons International Film Festival. She holds a BS in Information Technology (RPI), an MFA in Creative Nonfiction (Seattle Pacific), and an MA in Humanities and Social Thought (NYU). Haiver is her spouse of nearly twenty years. The session covers: awards and their function, the festival calendar, the critic's role, the business of film, and the specific territory of experimental and art video.

---

## Overview

The conversation moves across two interlinked arguments: that good criticism is a practice of generous attention rather than judgment, and that the material conditions of the film industry — money, platforms, studios, unions — are inseparable from understanding the art itself. A third thread emerges throughout: the particular power of place-based, embodied viewing in a world where cinema is becoming platform-agnostic. The final section offers practical advice for artists with unusual backgrounds entering creative fields.

---

## 1. Awards: Ranking vs. Discovery

The session opens during Oscar season, which Wilkinson frames immediately against the dominant misconception that awards are about ranking artworks against each other:

> "How do you judge two things that are kind of unlike one another to be like better than one another? It's not a race. It's not sports."

**What awards actually do:**
- Curate and surface work that might not otherwise reach audiences — particularly important for international films seeking US distribution
- Boost careers, especially for below-the-line workers (costume design, production design, visual effects, sound) who have fewer public-facing opportunities
- Signal: "This work is worth your time" in an oversaturated landscape

**What awards do badly:**
- Reduce art to competition, encouraging political-style campaigning and "dirty tricks"
- Create pressure that can distort creative decisions during production

A new Casting category at the Oscars this year marks the first formal recognition for casting directors — a discipline invisible to audiences that shapes everything.

For Wilkinson, Oscar night at the NYT involves 80+ staff working simultaneously across venues, outlets, and formats — the scale of a major election night.

## 2. The Film Festival Calendar

The festival year follows a loose seasonal rhythm that shapes how films reach audiences and critics:

| Period | What Happens |
|--------|-------------|
| January–May | Regular releases; horror and low-budget films dominate early months |
| May | Cannes — major commercial and prestige premieres; has a market for funding/distribution |
| Summer | Hollywood blockbusters, franchise films |
| September | Venice, Toronto, Telluride — concentrated three-week window when Oscar-hopeful films launch |
| Thanksgiving–December | Family films, holiday releases, Oscar qualifying deadline (US release required by Dec 31) |
| Early March | Oscars ceremony |

**Key festivals by character:**
- **Cannes** — major prestige and commercial; has its own market where filmmakers meet distributors and funders
- **Sundance** — "the premier independent film festival in the world"; dedicated to independent and experimental work; includes a *Next* section (experimental fiction/documentary hybrids, AI films) and a *Frontiers* section (VR, immersive, art projects)
- **Venice, Berlin** — known for experimental and art film; Venice has an immersive sidebar for VR and interactive projects
- **Toronto** — growing market component alongside prestige programming
- **Mid-tier local festivals** — often deeply curated, passionate, less funded; excellent for emerging makers

**For emerging makers, mid-tier festivals offer:**
- Audiences seeing your work
- Meeting potential collaborators and funders
- Being written about, even locally
- Developing language around the work through Q&As
- Networking — parties, coffee hours, informal conversations

Documentary festivals are especially useful for nonfiction or hybrid work. Film and art connect naturally; if a local festival lacks an art component, it's worth approaching organizers directly.

## 3. The Role of Film Critics

**The biggest misconception:** Critics don't like movies.

> "I think our job is to expand movies, give context to movies, explore movies. I wouldn't do this job if I didn't love movies passionately."

A review is not a verdict. Wilkinson frames her goal as opening up a work for readers so they can explore their own response:

> "When you read my review, my goal is not for you to get to the end and know what I think. I want you to have a better sense of what you think after you finish reading what I wrote."

The image she returns to: "Come with me. Let's walk around inside this thing and see what it has to say to us."

**What makes a good critic:**

- **Curiosity and humility** — assume the problem may be you, not the artist:
  > "If I imagine that art is the way an artist processes the world, then I have to imagine that when someone processes the world and I don't understand it, that the problem may not be the artist. I am here to learn."

- **Generosity as method** — treat the work as a gift:
  > "I try to imagine that the film is a gift being given to me."
  > "I want to assume that you were trying to make a good movie. Most people try to make good art. They're not trying to make bad art."

- **Omnivorous reference** — draw from philosophy, music, literature, culture:
  > "I want to be able to bring in a philosophical reference, but I want to be able to maybe quote Taylor Swift."

- **Taking things on their own terms:**
  > "I need to take it on its own terms and not ask it to be something other than what it is. Just like I wouldn't ask a person to be a different person in order to be acceptable to me."

**Jerry Saltz's formulation** (which Wilkinson cites as the right entry point for difficult work): *"What would I like about this if I were the kind of person who liked it?"*

## 4. Criticism and Critique: Shared Ground

The skill set of a critic and the skill set of a workshop reader or peer reviewer share the same core structure:

- Pay close attention to what a work is *trying* to be
- Attend to your own response — how does this make you feel?
- Distinguish between a personal reaction and something structural in the work
- Don't ask art to be something other than what it is
- Make honesty and generosity coexist

This is the method for a pan as much as a rave. The difference is only in conclusion, not in approach.

## 5. Panning: When Negative Reviews Are Appropriate

Wilkinson draws a clear line:

**Won't pan:** First films or small independent films without sufficient platform. There is no ethical reason to deploy the reach of the NYT against something that can't defend itself.

**Will pan:** "Critic-proof" movies — franchise blockbusters making billions regardless of any review. Studios panning their own IP doesn't hurt.

The key when panning is that it must be fun to read:

> "Nobody is going to read my review if I'm just like, 'this is bad.' If you're going to trash it, we should all have fun with it."

Her *The Snowman* review — a film she called "transcendently awful" — prompted a note from the screenwriter: "Thank you, this is so funny. You're totally right."

## 6. The Business of Film — Inseparable from the Art

Wilkinson's second major argument: understanding the material conditions of film production is part of understanding the work.

> "All of our art is produced in a highly capitalist system. We don't have government support basically at all."

**The Netflix documentary format as case study:** Every Netflix documentary now opens with a self-trailer — subject addresses camera, provocative line, fast clip montage, title card, then restarts at the beginning. This is a platform mandate, not a filmmaker choice. Streaming data showed viewership dropping off within three minutes; the format is a business response.

> "If I didn't know the business reason, I might just criticize the film for doing that. It's much more interesting to know why it's happening and to know that this is a studio choice, not a filmmaker choice."

**Other structural conditions:**
- Sequels and reboots are not failures of imagination — they are studio commercial decisions. Studios will not fund original stories at comparable budgets.
- Labor unions (writers, actors, directors, crew) are currently negotiating contracts that directly affect what can be made
- AI anxiety is not about the technology itself (film has always been tech-driven) but about cutting out the bottom rungs of the ladder: background actors, entry-level effects workers, color timers, sound assistants. Removing those rungs makes the industry inaccessible to people without existing means.

**Historical grounding:** John Gregory Dunn's embedded journalism at 20th Century Fox in the early 1970s — documented in *The Studio* — shows the same commercial pressures, audience testing, reshoot culture, and expensive talent negotiations that define the industry today. Nothing has fundamentally changed; the scale has shifted.

**Current state of the industry (2026):** "Everybody's scared." Streaming platforms massively overexpanded during COVID, are now contracting. Jobs disappearing. Warner Brothers facing acquisition by Paramount — viewed as poor outcome — as major films (*Sinners*, *One Battle After Another*) win Best Picture.

**The upside of streaming:** Democratizes access to world cinema. Gen Z viewers are more versed in international film than any previous generation, experiencing other cultures' storytelling regularly. The same platforms cutting jobs are also enabling broader cultural contact.

## 7. Documentary Film and Experimental Work

**Festival documentary versus Ken Burns:** Festivals favor observational, experimental, and formally innovative nonfiction — not the conventional educational documentary model. Wilkinson's favorite festival memory: a documentary about a single cow in Switzerland.

> "Show a slice of life rather than telling people what to think."

**Laura Poitras** (Wilkinson's favorite documentarian/artist): Works across documentary and art film without distinguishing between them. *Citizen Four* and *All the Beauty and the Bloodshed* — activist, documentarian, artist simultaneously.

## 8. Experimental Film and Art Video

This section directly addresses artists working with moving image:

**The radical quality of place-based viewing:**

> "There's something very radical about" requiring viewers to be physically present "in a world that is turning cinema into something that's platform agnostic."

Art video and installation demand the kind of viewing that domestic streaming removes. Two specific examples:

- **Christian McCauley's *The Clock* (MoMA):** You must be there. You wait in line, sit in a room, watch real time pass inside the work. You cannot replicate this at home.
- **MoMA's redesign:** Integrated cinema into gallery spaces to make explicit that moving image is part of the evolution of fine art — not a separate discipline.

**The body and the screen:**

> "It's good for us to be smaller than the movie. That's actually physically good for us."

Large-scale projections and immersive environments do something physiologically that small screens cannot. We're conditioned to screens — desensitized to the scale relationship. Art filmmakers and video artists get to shift and shock that conditioning, to rearrange, as she puts it, "the internal furniture."

**Pseudonarrative:** The term Wilkinson uses for work that has narrative structure (characters, movement, duration) but no plot and no story arc. A meta-narrative exists, but meaning isn't carried by events.

> "The point is NOT what happens to the characters. It's 'writing with light' — processing elements of life and identity through the screen."

Example: **A. Young Kim's *Delivery Dancer* (MoMA PS1)** — 90 minutes across three rooms, figures, dance movements, a statement about commerce and place. You sink into it as art the way you look at a painting. Like Tarkovsky: no plot is happening; it mimics the texture of life.

A Venice Biennale piece she couldn't stop returning to — people crawling over sand dunes for 30 minutes — changed something in how she sees. She watched it three times and couldn't recall the artist's name, but the internal shift persisted:

> "That is kind of what I'm thinking about with experimental film — it shifts how we watch the rest of our lives. Something very special and unique and interesting about what the artist gets to do."

## 9. Unusual Backgrounds as Asset

The final section turns practical. Wilkinson has a BS in Information Technology from 2005 — an engineering degree that seemed unrelated to film criticism but now feels essential.

> "The weirder you look on paper, the better off you are when you're trying to apply for a residency or a grant or something."

**What her tech training gave her:** The habit of breaking a problem into its smallest parts and solving incrementally. A way of thinking, not just a skill set. The combination of technical background, arts training, and critical writing practice is unusual in her field — that unusualness is the asset.

> "Don't be afraid to talk about it. It's an asset, not a liability."

For students with non-arts backgrounds, unusual professional histories, or surprising combinations of skills: those combinations are not apologies. They're leverage.

## Key Takeaways

- **Awards curate; they don't rank.** Their value is discovery and career amplification, not resolving aesthetic questions
- **The festival system is learnable.** Mid-tier local festivals are often better for emerging makers than blockbuster prestige events — go, meet people, develop language for the work
- **Good criticism opens, not closes.** The goal is to help readers understand their own response, not to deliver a verdict
- **Generosity is a method, not a personality trait.** Assume the artist was trying to make something good; take the work on its own terms before asking what it failed to do
- **The business is part of the art.** Knowing why a Netflix documentary opens the way it does matters as much as knowing how to read what it does cinematically
- **Place-based viewing is radical now.** Requiring physical presence — in the gallery, the theater, the installation — is a genuine formal and political position in a platform-agnostic era
- **Pseudonarrative is its own genre.** Moving image work that has structure without plot asks viewers to receive experience rather than story — and that asks a different kind of attention
- **Unusual backgrounds are assets.** Whatever you brought to this practice from another field is part of what makes you specific

---

## Reflection Questions

1. **Awards and visibility:** What is the equivalent of "getting written about" in your medium? What curatorial mechanisms — prizes, residencies, publications, festivals — exist to surface work in your field, and how do you understand their function?

2. **Generosity as method:** Think of a work you've encountered (in any medium) that you struggled with or disliked. Can you apply Saltz's question — "What would I like about this if I were the kind of person who liked it?" — and follow it? What opens up?

3. **The business behind the form:** In your own practice, what material conditions — budget, platform, venue, deadline — have shaped formal decisions? Are those constraints visible in the work? Should they be?

4. **Place-based experience:** Does your work ask viewers to be present somewhere specific, or is it platform-agnostic? Is that a deliberate position? What would requiring physical presence do to or for the work?

5. **Unusual background:** What did you bring to your art practice from somewhere else — a job, a field of study, a life experience? How does that background create angles of vision your practice doesn't yet use fully?
