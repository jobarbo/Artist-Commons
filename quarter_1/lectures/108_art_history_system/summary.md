# Art History: Systems Learn Uncertainty
## Chance, Randomness, and Generative Art (Session 2)

**Speaker:** Dr. Polina Rud  
**Context:** Second art history lecture of the quarter, focused on the practice of visiting artists in residence Jen and Kevin McCoy. The lecture connects art-historical experiments with chance to contemporary generative art and reframes how we relate to uncertainty in practice.

---

## Overview

This lecture traces how human cultures have negotiated **uncertainty**—the absence of guarantees that effort will succeed or that past decisions were right—and how that relationship evolved from ritual and faith to calculation, systems, and algorithms. **Generative art** is framed not as a style or technology but as a **way of thinking**: the constant oscillation between control and unpredictability, between structure and chance, and the question of how much uncertainty we allow into our lives and our work.

The narrative is organized in four parts: (1) **The Loss of Guarantees**, (2) **From Absurdity to Instruction**, (3) **When Error Becomes Information**, and (4) **Designing Systems**. A central thread: *generative art is about the boundary between control and unpredictability.*

---

## Part 1: The Loss of Guarantees

### Why Guarantees Matter

When we speak about uncertainty, we are speaking about **guarantees**—and their absence. To soften the tension with the unknown, people have always looked for something to lean on: the roll of the dice, the oracle, or an attempt to connect with something beyond logic and calculation. Across history, humans have searched for reference points to understand what can be controlled and how much uncertainty they are willing to allow.

### Pre-Modern Relations to Chance

**Paleolithic and ancient pagan cultures:** Chance was **something to listen to**, not to suppress. These forces were understood as creative, natural, or spiritual tools to integrate into practice. In Paleolithic caves (Lascaux, Altamira), artists did not dominate the surface; they worked *with* it—cracks, bulges, irregularities in the wall shaped animal figures. **The environment was a co-creative force.**

**Aristotle — Technē and Tychē:** In the *Physics*, Aristotle distinguishes:

- **Tychē** — chance as an *accidental cause*: what happens outside deliberate intention but still within the realm of what could be otherwise.
- **Technē** — a form of *knowing*, the capacity to produce artifacts through the conscious application of causes (material, formal, efficient, final).

Technē does not eliminate chance; it **intervenes** in it. The maker uses tychē as an incidental cause; unpredictability is **integrated in the process**. This can be read as a kind of **proto-theory of generativity**—two and a half millennia before computers.

### Canon and Faith: Innovation as Heresy

**Byzantine iconography (e.g., Andrei Rublev):** Proportion, colors, and even the sequence of brushstrokes were prescribed. The icon was not meant to be the artist’s interpretation; it functioned as **transmission of a sacred image**. **Innovation was heresy.**

**Islamic art:** Value lay in mathematical precision, symmetry, continuity. Patterns were meant to dissolve the individual ego into a timeless order. In both cases, **chance was not invited**—uncertainty was resolved in advance through faith.

### The Collapse of Guarantees

**The plague** wiped out the righteous and the sinful without distinction. The world revealed itself as **unpredictable**; the “transparent” divine plan was no longer transparent.

**Printing** accelerated thought, made it reproducible, mobile, and vulnerable to error. Truth was no longer singular or fixed; in multiplication it lost its absolute center.

**The figure of the author changes:** The author becomes someone who **assumes responsibility**. The name matters more than lineage because the name carries risk. The author is the one whose thought might be wrong, whose error can no longer be justified by appealing to divine or traditional authority. **The human being is forced to confront uncertainty directly.** Every action becomes an experiment; the artist’s gesture becomes a risk.

### Giotto and the Open Work

Working in **fresco**, Giotto operated under **irreversibility**: once pigment touches wet plaster, it cannot be corrected; the result cannot be fully calculated in advance. In the Scrovegni Chapel (c. 1305), he departs from the Byzantine canon—introducing volume, spatial depth, individual emotion, human poses that did not exist before. The scenes are no longer closed and doctrinally fixed; **they become open to interpretation**. Meaning is no longer fully guaranteed. In this sense, Giotto’s practice anticipates conceptual art: the work does not dictate a single stable meaning but creates **conditions for meaning to emerge** in interaction with the viewer. *(If Giotto and Sol LeWitt ever met, they’d be surprised how well they agree.)*

### Systems Thinking Before Modern Science

**Cornelius Agrippa (1533), *Three Books of Occult Philosophy* — *magia sympathica*:** A vision of the world as a **network of hidden correspondences**, where causality is not linear but **distributed**. Events do not happen because of a single origin but because a configuration of relations comes together at a specific moment. Causality spreads across the system. This way of thinking connects to what we now call **systems thinking**: the whole cannot be reduced to the sum of its parts; the behavior of a system cannot be fully predicted by analyzing one element in isolation. **Chance becomes not the opposite of order, but a signal of complexity.**

### Modernity: Uncertainty as Calculation

**Pascal and Fermat (mid-17th century):** In their correspondence on the “problem of points” (gambling), **risk unexpectedly turns into an object of reason**—something that can be analyzed, broken down, formalized. For the first time, uncertainty is something that can be **worked with computationally**.

**After the Great Fire of London:** When risk can be counted, priced, and compared, new institutions emerge—**insurance companies**, **commodities**, **futures**. Randomness becomes a commodity; risk is evaluated; the future is translated into percentages; **uncertainty is built into calculation**. This logic penetrates culture: the art market begins to function as a space of speculation (e.g., Rembrandt entangled in financial expectations and investment risk).

**Mozart and musical dice games:** Randomness becomes an **explicit co-author**. The composer defines rules, tables, grammar; the sequence is decided by a throw of the die. The author no longer fully controls the outcome but **designs systems within which different results can occur**. Society learns to live without guarantees by embedding chance into science, economics, art, and life.

### Romanticism and the Collapse of Rational Guarantees

**19th century:** Romanticism and the crisis of the Enlightenment expose the illusion of rational guarantees at the level of lived experience. In Caspar David Friedrich’s paintings, the human figure appears tiny and fragile before nature. The French Revolution—conceived as a triumph of reason—does not lead to stable human order. Calculation continues, but the **promise of meaning collapses**.

**Nietzsche, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1882) — “God is dead”:** Not primarily a theological claim but a diagnosis of the **collapse of absolute foundations of truth and meaning**. Interpretation becomes unstable; the author loses final authority. Uncertainty evolves from something managed by formulas into a **fundamental void**.

### Quantum Physics: Uncertainty as Structural

**Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle:** It is impossible to determine both position and momentum of a particle with complete precision; the more accurately one is measured, the less certain the other becomes. **The observer is no longer outside the system** neutrally recording facts but becomes **part of the process**. Properties do not always exist in a fully definite state before observation; **reality emerges through interaction**.

**Bohr’s principle of complementarity:** Quantum phenomena cannot be captured by a single universal model. In some experiments particles behave like waves, in others like particles; these descriptions contradict each other and cannot be applied simultaneously, yet **both are necessary**. Neither is more true than the other. Uncertainty is no longer something to be calculated away; it becomes **structural**. Reality resists a single explanation, a single viewpoint—and thus a single authoritative meaning.

**Summary of Part 1:** Uncertainty is a constant; our *ways of relating* to it change—from ritual and faith, to calculation, to systems and algorithms. Chance is not always the opposite of order; it shifts from creative force, to suppressed, to designed into systems. Authorship emerges together with risk. Modernity turns uncertainty into calculation (probability, insurance, speculation) without eliminating existential doubt. Scientific revolutions dismantle determinism and make the observer part of the system.

## Part 2: From Absurdity to Instruction

### Authority as Threat

Against the background of scientific and cultural shift, **authority no longer looks like protection; it starts to feel like a threat**. Any voice that claims to know how things should be raises suspicion. The 20th century—world wars, gas chambers, mass bombing, atomic weapons—intensifies a sense of **absurdity**. **Reality is not obliged to be meaningful.** It is at this point that **Dada** emerges.

### Dada: Chance as Rupture

**Hans (Jean) Arp,** working in Zurich during the First World War (where Joyce and Lenin were also present): If the world is absurd, art should not pretend to be meaningful. **Chance and play** are placed at the center of human activity as an **antithesis to war and ideology**. If language has served propaganda and command, it must be broken. If the author claims control, the author must be removed. **Chance becomes a way to liberate art from the violence of imposed meanings.**

**Tristan Tzara:** Proposes making poems by cutting words from newspapers and drawing them at random. **Formal instruction replaces inspiration**; the artwork emerges as the result of a procedure.

**Marcel Duchamp — *Trois Stoppages Étalon* (3 Standard Stoppages):** Drops three threads onto canvas and fixes their accidental fall as **new units of measurement**. With this gesture he shows that **the very foundations of order can be arbitrary**. Art stops representing or copying the world and **begins to design its own rules**. The standard no longer measures the world; **the standard emerges from the world.**

### Fluxus: Chance as Consequence of Rules

In the 1960s, working with chance changes. If for Dada chance was primarily a **gesture of rupture**—breaking language, authority, the illusion of meaning—within **Fluxus** it becomes the **consequence of the rule**. Fluxus works are built on very strict, extremely simple rules and minimal actions, yet **outcomes can be totally different every time**—dependent on context, performer, environment.

**John Cage:** Influenced by Zen (D. T. Suzuki) and the *I Ching*, Cage uses coin tosses as a working method for composition—duration, pitches, silences, structure. He is not interested in what he wants to say but in **what is already sounding in the world**. Music does not need interpretation; it is not obliged to be meaningful or expressive; it simply exists. **Chance becomes a method of removing authorial control**, a way of refusing the ego.

**4′33″:** The performer comes on stage and does not play a single note. The hall fills with coughing, breathing, creaking chairs, street noise. **Music becomes the environment itself.**

**Fluxus event scores (Yoko Ono, George Brecht):** Short instructions—e.g., “Listen to your heartbeat,” “Light a match, imagine, watch it burn.” They do **not** prescribe a result; they **initiate an event**. Anyone can perform them; every realization will be different. Chance arises as a side effect of life, time, body, presence, environment. **Authorship dissolves**; the work is repeatable but never exactly reproducible.

### Oulipo: Constraint as Freedom

**Oulipo** approaches instructions as **constraint**. They work from the paradox: **The stricter the rule, the more freedom it generates.**

- **Raymond Queneau — *One Hundred Thousand Billion Poems*:** Ten sonnets in which each line can be combined with any other; the reader generates the text. The author writes the structure; the reader generates. This is already very close to an algorithm.
- **Georges Perec — *La Disparition*:** A novel written entirely without the letter “e” (the most frequent letter in French). The experiment raises the question: How does thinking reorganize itself under the pressure of the rule?

### The Key Shift

**The artist no longer throws the die personally.** The artist **designs a field in which reality itself inevitably throws the die**. The artist becomes a **programmer of situations**. The result does not pre-exist; it **emerges**. Authority loses legitimacy; art replaces inspiration with **constraint** and authorship with **systems**. Chance is treated not as error but as an **inbuilt parameter**.

**Summary of Part 2:** After the catastrophes of the 20th century, authority loses legitimacy. Dada introduces chance as rupture; Fluxus transforms chance into a consequence of rules. Authorship dissolves into executable situations. Constraint replaces inspiration; the artist becomes a designer of systems. Outcomes do not pre-exist; they emerge through execution. Chance is a built-in parameter of the work.

## Part 3: When Error Becomes Information

### Randomness as Source of Knowledge

A crucial mid-20th-century shift: **randomness starts to be seen as a source of knowledge about the system itself**—where it can be activated, how it behaves—not only as error or inbuilt parameter. This emerges from deep scientific transformation in the 1940s–50s.

### Cybernetics

**Cybernetics** appears as the science of control and self-regulation in complex systems (machines, organisms, social processes). Core insight: **No system can ever be fully controlled.** Control is only possible through **feedback**, and feedback only exists when a system **deviates** from expected behavior. **Through deviation, the system learns what is happening and adjusts.** Without deviation, the system is effectively blind. **Error is no longer failure; it becomes information.** Randomness and instability are not the opposite of order; they are **conditions**. Control is no longer imagined as the elimination of uncertainty but as **the capacity to work with it**.

### Vera Molnár and the *Machine Imaginaire*

**Vera Molnár** works with what she calls a “manual algorithm”—drawing cards, tables of random numbers, grids, repetitions. Her early 1960s works are essentially **programming with a pencil**. She deliberately dismantles the **illusion of the perfect machine** by introducing the concept of the **machine imaginaire** (imaginary machine): not a physical device but a conceptual model—a set of rules, repetitions, transformations that can be executed without a computer. Her idea of **controlled chance** functions as an artistic equivalent of **cybernetic feedback**. Minimal errors, microscopic shifts, slight inaccuracies do not destroy the system; **they make the system visible**. The algorithm begins to respond to its own deviations like a self-regulating system for which instability is a condition of functioning.

### Information Theory

In classical thinking, **noise** is what interferes with meaning and must be eliminated. **Information theory** shows that noise can never be fully removed—it is always present in the channel—and, crucially, **without noise, the signal itself cannot be measured**. Noise stops being evil and becomes something the system must work with, sometimes a source of additional information. **Chance is no longer what threatens the system; it is what allows the system to be known.**

### Arte Generativo (Argentina, 1960)

At the intersection of systems thinking, rejection of representation, and the new understanding of form as process, **Arte Generativo** emerges. **Eduardo Mac Entyre** and **Miguel Ángel Vidal** (with theorist Ignacio Pirovano) publish the **Arte Generativo manifesto** and present the movement in an exhibition at Galería Van Riel. In the manifesto they criticize Op art for producing mere visual tricks. What they demand: **the artwork should not be the result, but the visible operation of a system**—something the viewer can mentally reconstruct. They display works **in series** rather than as isolated objects: not “here is the artwork,” but **“here is a range of possible states.”** Each piece is one realization of the rule. Generativity is manual but rigorously procedural: the artist defines the system of actions but does not control specific form. Through repetition, displacement, interference, overlay, elements behave like a system—optical vibrations, illusions of depth and movement.

### Sol LeWitt: The Idea as Machine

**Sol LeWitt**, “Paragraphs on Conceptual Art”: **“The idea becomes a machine that produces art.”** He designs a system that then operates on its own. For wall drawings he writes instructions to be executed by assistants or museum staff (e.g., draw 10,000 lines of random lengths). He does not decide where each line will be, what it looks like, or how it falls—that is determined by the performer or by physical limitations of the space.

**Why pair Mac Entyre and LeWitt?** To avoid a simplified narrative where instructions come first, then ideas, then systems—and visual form appears as secondary (a largely Anglo-French narrative where form follows text). **Mac Entyre solves the same problem without instructions.** He demonstrates that it is possible to think systemically, to work with rules, **without relying on text, language, or explicit directives**. Generative art, at its core, **thinks in rules embodied in forms**—lines, modules, transformations—rather than in words. Generative art does not emerge from instructions as such; it emerges from **working with form itself**.

**LeWitt vs. Fluxus instructions:**

- **LeWitt:** The instruction functions as a **mechanism for precise transmission of an idea**. Execution may vary, but it must remain a correct realization of the underlying algorithm. Randomness (e.g., lines of random length) is delegated and **strictly bounded by the system**. The instruction operates as an **algorithm**; the author is the architect of a **space of possible states**.
- **Fluxus:** The instruction does **not** realize an idea; it **initiates an event**. Event scores do not imply a “correct” outcome; they are not evaluated by fidelity to original intention. There is no proper execution.

**Summary of Part 3:** Mid-20th century science reframes randomness. In cybernetics and information theory, error and noise become sources of knowledge. Systems regulate themselves through deviation; instability is a condition of order. The artwork becomes the visible operation of a system, not a finished image. The work exists as a range of possible states; series replace unique objects. The idea becomes a machine; authorship moves from execution to system design. LeWitt’s instructions are algorithms aimed at reproducibility; Fluxus instructions trigger events without a “correct” outcome.

## Part 4: Designing Systems

### Generative Art as a Way of Thinking

**Generative art is not defined by style, technology, or historical period; it is defined by a mode of thinking.** The artist does not design a form but a **process**. Procedure is the key feature; **chance does not cancel the rule—it operates inside it.** Generative art is often described as **unpredictability within constraints**.

### Types of Randomness (Terminology)

- **Aleatoric:** Pure chance (e.g., a dice throw)—a single random act directly determines the outcome. “I don’t choose; chance chooses instead of me.”
- **Stochastic:** Probability distributions. The system chooses within a defined range (e.g., a line may deviate most often by 1–2°, more rarely by 10°, almost never by 90°).
- **Pseudorandomness:** Generated by an algorithm rather than true chaos. Same initial parameter → same result every time.
- **Emergence:** No random choice is added, yet the result appears unpredictable because the system is too complex—**interactions within the system become opaque.**

**Generative art is almost never about chaos; it is about the boundary between control and unpredictability.**

### Types of Generative Systems

Distinguished not as schools or movements but as **types of systems** artists work with:

| Type | Focus | Key question |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **Procedural** | Procedures, steps, instructions, actions over time | What sequence of actions does the artist set in motion? |
| **Algorithmic** | Formalized rules, repetition, transformation, code (or “mental code”) | What rules govern the form? |
| **Cellular automata / Agent-based** | Local rules, interactions between elements, emergence of global patterns | How does complex behavior arise from simple rules? |
| **Data-driven** | Data as material—transforming, visualizing, abstracting streams | What happens when the world becomes input? |
| **Hybrid (e.g. on-chain)** | NFTs, blockchain-based works | What happens when social and economic rules are embedded in the generative process? |

### Algorithmic Generative Art

When computers and the graphic plotter appear, the system stops being imaginary or manual and becomes an **executable machine**. A visual factory of variations: one algorithm can produce many realizations. Form is often generated by **purely deterministic rules**; same input → same output. **The image is only evidence that the procedure ran.** Transparency, repeatability, and reproducibility become central. Complexity can arise from the strict repetition of simple operations, not from a random gesture.

**Georg Nees — *Schotter* (1968–70):** Order begins to “loosen” from within a rule; the grid remains but the algorithm introduces random deviations that increase as the composition unfolds.

**Frieder Nake — *Hommage à Paul Klee* (1965):** The image is a **trace of a computable process** that can be repeated and compared logically.

**Recursion:** A rule applied repeatedly to the result of its own operation. The system does not invent new instructions; it keeps executing the same rule. **Fractals** (e.g., the Mandelbrot set): a simple formula, no image or aesthetic intention, yet when iterated recursively produces infinitely complex structure—**an unavoidable consequence of the rule applied to itself.**

**Roman Verostko:** Uses **L-systems** (Lindenmayer systems)—formal rewriting systems—so that recursive rewritings produce branching structures resembling growth and nature. **Form does not depict nature; it emerges the way nature emerges**, through repeated application of the same law at different scales.

**Manfred Mohr:** Uses recursion not to simulate growth but to **systematically explore the space of possible states** of a formal system. Extremely restrictive elements (lines, angles, cubes); by minimizing vocabulary he makes the underlying logic fully legible. **Recursion exhausts the rule**—asking about limits.

**Perlin noise / Jared Tarbell:** Noise functions (e.g., Perlin noise) model natural irregularities (clouds, water, terrain). Output appears chaotic but the process is **fully deterministic**. What looks like chaos is layered, mathematically controlled variation. **The rule disguises itself as chaos.** In Tarbell’s *Substrate* series, multi-octave Perlin noise applied to a grid produces structures reminiscent of coral, mineral growth, crystalline formations—**deterministic deviations**, not random accidents.

### Cellular Automata

**Cellular automata** take generative thinking further: if algorithmic art shows that strict rules can produce form, cellular automata show that **rules can produce behavior**. Time enters the system; the work is no longer a static object but a **world unfolding**.

Setup: a grid of cells, each with a minimal state (e.g., alive/dead, on/off). Each cell follows a **local rule**—it looks only at immediate neighbors and updates accordingly. **No cell has knowledge of the whole;** there is no central control, no global plan, no “view from above.” Yet from these **microscopic rules**, **macroscopic structure emerges**. This is **emergence**: global behavior arising from local interactions. (Gestalt psychology describes the same intuition from the perspective of perception: the whole is not reducible to the sum of its parts.)

**John Conway — *Game of Life* (1970):** Three rules: a living cell dies if too few or too many neighbors; a dead cell becomes alive if exactly three neighbors. From these rules, unexpected entities appear—oscillators, moving forms (gliders), self-replicating patterns (glider guns, puffers). The Game of Life is **computationally universal** (can simulate any computer). **Behavior resembling life**, once attributed to intention and biological essence, turns out to be possible **without author and without plan**—no design, no goal, only rule and repetition.

Artists recognize the aesthetic potential: cellular automata visualize time, growth, mutation, collapse. **Form is temporal, not static.** Composition is replaced by growth; the artwork is a process, not an object; meaning emerges rather than being imposed. The artist relinquishes global control—defining initial conditions and local behavior but not the final result.

**Casey Reas** (co-creator of Processing): In *Process* series, simple behavioral rules for elements that interact locally; form emerges from interaction. **Jonathan McCabe:** Complex multistate cellular automata where color functions as state; forms resemble biological tissues, corals. Connects to **Alan Turing**, “The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis”—how complex biological patterns emerge from initially uniform conditions. Emergence is not a metaphor borrowed from biology but a **shared structural principle**.

### Data-Driven Generative Art

**Question shifts:** From “What can emerge from simple rules?” to “How can existing reality be perceived differently?” The system is no longer autonomous; **its fuel is data**—measurements, archives, streams. **Time:** In cellular automata, time is internal (each step transforms the world from within). In data-driven work, time is often a **property of the data**—already recorded; the artwork scrolls, remixes, filters, reveals. **Authorship:** In cellular automata the artist gives up control and accepts emergence; in data-driven work **control partially returns**—the artist chooses which data to show and how to map it. Less systemic surprise, more responsibility for translation.

**Jer Thorp:** Among the first to treat data visualization as artistic practice—airline routes, social networks, databases turned into visual systems that reveal hidden structures. Data visualization as a **bridge between the analytical and the poetic**; data are not merely illustrated but **reorganized in new modes of thinking**.

**Robert Hodgin:** Environment plays an active role—rivers, flowers, sound, natural dynamics. He does not represent nature visually but works with **data generated by the environment**, translating signals into the behavior of a generative system. What enters the work is not the river itself but its velocity, fluctuations; **nature functions as co-creative force through translation**.

**Ryoji Ikeda:** Opposite pole—pure numerical structures: high-frequency sound, flickering light, grids, synchronized streams of digits, binary sequences. The viewer is confronted with scale, speed, precision—**an aesthetic of the limit**, where data are experienced as sublime, almost unbearable.

In all these cases, **art ceases to be a single object and becomes a field of possible states.**

### Hybrid / On-Chain Generative Art

By the early 2020s, generative art reaches a point where **system autonomy is radicalized**. Previously the algorithm existed as something external (a file, a program, an instruction). **With on-chain generative art, the code becomes the very site of the work’s existence.** On platforms such as **Art Blocks** (founded by Erick Calderon) and **FXHash**, the artwork’s algorithm is written directly onto the **blockchain**. The code is independent of the artist, gallery, or institution and **cannot be altered retroactively**.

**Ontological shift:**

- The artwork **does not exist until minting.** The collector acquires the right to **activate** the system; at the moment of transaction the algorithm receives a random seed and produces a **concrete realization for the first time**. This form appears once and can never be repeated.
- The artist **gives up control** after publication; the code executes and authenticates itself. Continuation of LeWitt’s “art as a machine”—but now the machine is **legally independent** from its author.
- **Value shifts to the system.** What is monetized is not the image but the rule; the collector trusts the process.
- **On-chain generative art is not an object or an AI model; it is an autonomous event—a law released into the world.**

There is **no external dataset**, no learning, prediction, or adaptation. The algorithm does not “know” the world; it **executes predefined rules**. Complexity is **procedural**, not cognitive.

**Examples:**

- **Tyler Hobbs (*Fidenza*):** Balance between control and uncertainty; algorithm tightly structured but parameter space rich so each realization appears unique. On-chain logic as a way to recover a sense of **handmade gesture without a hand**.
- **Dmitri Cherniak (*Ringers*):** Radical minimalism of rules—rotation, layering, limited forms produce complex, almost architectural compositions. **The stricter the rules, the richer the space of possible forms.**
- **William Mapan:** Procedural systems that generate grids, fields, structures; **expressive without becoming human gesture**—emotion emerges from the behavior of the system.
- **Emily Xie:** Generative system evoking handwriting, drawing, calligraphic movement; gestures produced by code. **Procedural autonomy can still produce work that feels deeply human** without returning control to the artist.

**Summary of Part 4:** Generative art is defined by a way of thinking. Different systems ask different questions (procedures, rules, emergence, data, on-chain autonomy). With computers, systems become executable; one algorithm can generate infinite variations; form is a trace of procedure. Across all these systems, the artwork is not an object but a **field of possible states**; meaning emerges through execution.

## Summary: Four Parts as One Arc

1. **The Loss of Guarantees** — From ritual and faith to calculation; authorship and risk; quantum physics and structural uncertainty.
2. **From Absurdity to Instruction** — Dada’s rupture; Fluxus and Cage’s rules; Oulipo’s constraints; the artist as programmer of situations.
3. **When Error Becomes Information** — Cybernetics, information theory, Molnár, Arte Generativo, LeWitt; the work as visible operation of a system.
4. **Designing Systems** — Types of randomness and types of generative systems; algorithmic art, cellular automata, data-driven work, on-chain art; the artwork as field of possible states and as autonomous event.

**Generative art is a way of thinking in systems** where algorithms become executable, artworks exist as fields of possible states, and **meaning emerges through interaction and execution rather than expression.**

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Uncertainty** is a constant in human history; what changes is how we relate to it—ritual, faith, calculation, systems, algorithms.
- **Chance** is not always the opposite of order; it can be a creative force, suppressed, or designed into systems.
- **Authorship** emerges with risk; when guarantees collapse, the artist becomes responsible for meaning—and for being wrong.
- **Modernity** turns uncertainty into calculation (probability, insurance, speculation) without eliminating existential doubt.
- **Scientific revolutions** (e.g., quantum physics) show uncertainty as structural and make the observer part of the system.
- **Dada** introduces chance as rupture; **Fluxus** makes chance a consequence of rules; **constraint** replaces inspiration; the artist becomes a designer of systems.
- **Mid-20th century:** Error and noise become sources of knowledge; the artwork becomes the visible operation of a system; the idea becomes a machine.
- **Generative art** is defined by a way of thinking, not by style or technology. The artwork is not an object but a **field of possible states**; meaning emerges through execution.
- **On-chain generative art:** The work is not an object but an **autonomous event**—a law launched once and remaining in the world.

## Studio Reflections

Questions to sit with in practice:

1. **What part of my work am I still trying to fully control—and why do I need that guarantee?** What fear would appear if I allowed that part to remain undecided?
2. **If my artwork were a system rather than an object, what rules would actually govern it—and which ones am I pretending are neutral?** What happens if I make those rules visible instead of hiding them inside form?
3. **Where in my process could an error stop being a failure and start becoming information?**
4. **What do I want to be the author of: the image, or the conditions under which the image emerges?** If I step back, what would continue acting in my place?
5. **How much uncertainty am I willing to allow—not aesthetically, but existentially—in my practice right now?**

## Afterword and Q&A Highlights

**Creation as co-creation:** The lecture closes by returning to the idea that creation was never a solitary act of expression; **the environment was a co-creative force** (caves of Altamira, the rock surface, pigments, light, movement). What we see today in generative and systems-based practice is a **continuation of this logic**, translated into technological context. The redistribution of authorship—artist, system, chance, environment—does not erase the author but **redistributes** agency. What changes is not the existence of these forces but **how much agency we grant them**.

**“All art is generative art”:** Frieder Nake (at the Generative Art Summit, Berlin) has said that all art is generative art—everything involves co-creation, chance, and randomness in some way. The lecture brings this full circle.

**I Ching and Heisenberg:** Cage’s use of the *I Ching* (and Zen) is not only about embracing chance but about **finding currents of the universe**—using a system that seems to use random forces to **reveal structure and order**. Heisenberg’s principle reveals that the universe is not strictly deterministic and that chance is built in; the *I Ching* suggests that through a system that engages “random” forces we can make unseen patterns visible.

**Art as event rather than object:** For generative artists (and artists generally): what would it do to our practice to think of what we make as an **event** rather than as an object? On-chain generative art is not an object but an **autonomous event**—a useful reframe for practice.

**Marina Abramović method:** The lecturer offered to use a deck of Marina Abramović instructions in the lecture chat—participants could pose questions (about practice, appropriation, blockages, etc.) and receive an **instruction** (not an answer) on what to do to find the answer, tying back to the theme of chance and instruction.

## Terminology Quick Reference

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Aleatoric** | Pure chance; single random act determines outcome. |
| **Stochastic** | Probability distributions; system chooses within a defined range. |
| **Pseudorandom** | Algorithm-generated; same seed → same result. |
| **Emergence** | Unpredictability from system complexity, not added randomness. |
| **Technē / Tychē** | Aristotle: craft/knowing vs. chance as accidental cause. |
| **Machine imaginaire** | Molnár: conceptual set of rules executable without a computer. |
| **Procedural** | Steps, instructions, actions unfolding over time. |
| **Algorithmic** | Formalized rules, repetition, transformation, code. |
| **Cellular automata** | Local rules, no central control; emergence of global patterns. |
| **Data-driven** | Data as material; world as input. |
| **On-chain** | Code as site of work; autonomous, unalterable execution. |
