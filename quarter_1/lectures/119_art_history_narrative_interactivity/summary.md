# Art History: Architectures of Narrative Interactivity

**Speaker:** Polina Rud — art historian  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** Second of two art history lectures on interactivity by Polina Rud. Companion to lecture 116 (Architectures of Interactivity), which covered the shift from static objects to dynamic fields and cybernetic feedback systems. This lecture focuses specifically on *narrative* — tracing how interactivity transformed who makes meaning and how, from communal oral storytelling through net art to contemporary game art. Includes companion Takeaways PDF with studio reflection prompts.

---

## Overview

The lecture advances a single argument across three major blocks: narrative has undergone a fundamental transformation from a transmission model (author delivers meaning to passive reader) to a participation model (viewer/player co-creates meaning through active traversal). This shift is not merely formal — it is political. Every interactive system embeds architectures of control; the artists surveyed here use interactivity not to celebrate technology but to interrogate it, making participants aware they are embedded in structures that constitute meaning itself.

**The throughline question:** *How does interactivity change who makes meaning, and how is that meaning made?*

---

## 1. The Problem with Linear Narrative

### From Oral to Print Culture

Before Aristotle, storytelling was communal and mutable — shaped by memory, retelling, and collective participation. Listeners were future storytellers. The story lived in exchange.

**Aristotle's *Poetics*** codified narrative as cathartic spectacle with a central hero. The listener's agency was erased; meaning became singular and fixed. Print culture further centralized this authority: beginning, middle, end; closure implies completeness; the reader becomes a passive consumer.

**Walter Benjamin ("The Storyteller," 1936):** Modernity killed communal storytelling — isolated us and stripped our power to co-create meaning. The narrator's loss is not merely aesthetic; it is a social impoverishment.

**The problem:** Traditional linear narrative elevates the author to what Barthes and Foucault call the "author-god" — a figure who controls interpretation and suppresses multiplicity of meanings. Derrida: interactive forms expose the "instability of meaning" — narratives are not closed systems but shifting constellations, temporary configurations shaped by choices, context, and consequences.

**Borges as precursor:** "The Garden of Forking Paths" constructs narratives that split into multiple co-existing possibilities without explicit interface. The text trains the mind to resist linearity, to imagine branching realities where each moment contains multiple directions simultaneously.

**Deleuze and Guattari's Rhizome:** Web-like structures without hierarchy where any point connects to any other. Meaning distributed across a network of relations — against root and branch.

**Key concept — Ergodic structures:** Narratives requiring effort and active participation to unfold. The illusion of closure belongs to linear narrative; questioning that illusion enables different modes of making.

## 2. Wave One: Non-Linear Experiments in Music, Theater, and Media (1950s–1980s)

### Experimental Music
**Karlheinz Stockhausen — *Klavierstück XI*:**  
A score with 19 separate fragments. Performers choose where to start, which fragments to follow — each performance unique. Instructions replace control; chance governs variability. The composer releases authorship over the final sequence.

### Theater and Performance
**Dick Higgins / Fluxus — "Danger Music No. 17":**  
Performers carry out mundane activities (telephone conversations, ordinary actions) at extreme volume. Scripted drama replaced with real-time, non-scripted action. Minimal guidelines — what Higgins called "loose agreements" — create space for interpretation that differs every time.

**Michael Kirby's non-matrixed performances:** Works that abandon fixed structure of time, place, and character. Flexible systems enable simultaneity, variability, and chance operations.

### Media and Kinetic Art
**Nam June Paik — "Random Access" (1963):**  
Removes magnetic tape from its recorder; attaches fragments to a gallery wall. Visitors wear a playback head that generates sound by touching the strips directly. No sequence. Participants navigate a "field of opportunities" — starting anywhere, repeating, skipping, creating unexpected combinations. The destruction of linearity is literal and material.

**Lynn Hershman Leeson — "Lorna" (1979–83):**  
Recreates a domestic space with a television and remote control. A fictional agoraphobic character; viewers select fragments of her daily existence via remote — routines, fears, reflections, isolation — assembling a psychological portrait. Different choices lead to different outcomes: escape or collapse. The remote control transforms from instrument of passive consumption into instrument of narrative construction.

**Grahame Weinbren — "The Earl King" (1980s, laser disc):**  
Draws from Goethe's poem and Freud's "burning child" dream — both exploring fatherhood, guilt, and the boundary between reality and hallucination. Viewers become film editors via touch screen, switching camera angles and accessing associative imagery. Weinbren distinguishes two crucial states:
- **Active state** — the viewer makes a selection
- **Subjunctive** — awareness that another choice would have created a different image

> "Another choice could have led to a different image. This is crucial because it destabilizes the idea of narrative inevitability."

**Jeffrey Shaw — "Legible City" (1989):**  
A stationary bicycle connected to a computer navigates a 3D virtual city composed entirely of text — letters that gradually reveal stories and historical references. Reading becomes movement. The participant constructs their own version through physical navigation. Meaning-making is transformed into locomotion.

## 3. Wave Two: Networked Narrative — Net Art (1990s)

### The Technological Shift

From computer networks to Videotex to the World Wide Web: information transforms from linear sequence to distributed, networked nodes. **Lev Manovich's database logic:** Traditional narrative organizes meaning through time; digital systems store elements as collections accessed in multiple ways. Meaning emerges from paths and choices, not predetermined sequence.

The critical context: these infrastructures were embedded in state technologies, corporate platforms, and protocols designed for administration, surveillance, and commercial distribution — not free expression. Net art that uses these systems is always already political.

### Key Net Art Works

**Eduardo Kac — "Reabracadabra" (1985, Brazilian Videotex):**  
An animated visual poem where letters rotate around a central "A" to form dynamic patterns. Users dial in via telephone to terminals and navigate through keyboard commands. The political dimension: Videotex was a state-controlled network under military dictatorship, used to distribute official propaganda. Kac infiltrates with non-linear, participatory poetry. Navigation becomes simultaneously an aesthetic gesture and a political act.

**Olia Lialina — "My Boyfriend Came Back From The War" (1996, hypertext browser):**  
A young woman meets a boyfriend returning from an unnamed war (widely read as the post-Soviet Chechen conflict). Non-linear dialogue moves from joy to betrayal and emotional rupture. Navigation via clicking words, GIF animations, icons — a blinking light bulb, a door. Each click splits the screen into smaller frames, fragmenting from a single image into dozens of tiny windows, eventually leaving only a black square.

The slow internet of 1996 (30 kilobytes) transforms technically: loading delays become meaningful pauses — the metaphor for what cannot be said after war, words stuck, communication impossible. The frame structure visualizes relational collapse; the relationship disintegrates into fragments as communication fails.

Lialina: *"If something is in the net it should speak in that language."*

The work was made during the final phase of the First Chechen War. A friend of Lialina's was killed. The narrative functions as archetype beyond any specific conflict.

**Susan Berkenheger — "Bubble Bath" (1996):**  
A critical work about early web culture. It functions only in Internet Explorer; any other browser shows a message suggesting the user "disguise themselves as a Microsoft occupier." The browser is revealed as a political environment shaped by corporate power.

Inside the work, the interface becomes unstable and deceptive: cursor drifts, links fail, pop-ups interrupt. The promised interactivity is deliberately undermined — not freedom but aggression. The reader is forced into struggle; every attempt to navigate meets disruption, delay, misdirection.

The work reflects its exact historical moment: Internet Explorer dominance, and the web already being shaped by corporate infrastructure before most users understood that was happening.

**Knowbotic Research — "Dialogue with Nobotics House" (mid-1990s):**  
Built on live data flows from Antarctic research stations; autonomous software agents (Nobots) connected in real time. The narrative is not a finished story but an emergent, reconfigurable environment shaped by visitor interaction. No beginning, middle, or end. The visitor participates in generating the story rather than receiving it.

## 4. Wave Three: Playable Narrative — Game Art (1960s–present)

### Origins in Military Technology

Interactivity emerges from an unexpected historical source: **military radar technology**. Radar introduced the first machines capable of perceiving signals, reacting, and requiring human interpretation — a feedback loop that is the earliest operational model of interactivity.

A recurring historical pattern: visualization technologies invested most heavily in **war and sexuality** — echoing Freud's suggestion that human culture is driven by Eros and Thanatos.

**Tennis for Two (1958):**  
Built using an analog computer connected to an oscilloscope (radar-related technology). Two players controlled paddles with button and knob; the screen showed a tennis court (horizontal line = ground, vertical = net, moving dot = ball). The system calculated ball trajectory. The crucial shift: **the screen was no longer a passive display but a responsive interface where action generates visual event**.

**Harun Farocki — *Serious Games* (2010–11):**  
(Not a game itself.) Documents how video game technology developed for entertainment was re-absorbed by the military for training, planning, and psychological therapy. The circle: games came from radar/military, developed by game industry, then looped back into military strategy and psychological conditioning.

### Game vs. Play

A key distinction running through the lecture:
- **Game:** defined by rules, goals, measurable outcomes, points, victories, completion
- **Play:** open-ended; exists for the experience itself; no need to win, optimize, or complete

Game art operates in the tension between these modes — using games to make visible the systems games assume.

### Critical Game Art Works

**Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley — "She Keeps Me Down Alive":**  
A pixel-based light gun arcade game with a custom bubblegum pink controller. Players move through a virtual landscape with avatars representing Black trans people — their testimonies, memories, and experiences are the core of the work. The game forces uncomfortable decisions about protection, solidarity, and responsibility. No explicit instructions; decisions rely on observing character interactions, audio cues, and context. Makes actions deliberately demanding and reflective.

**Molleindustria — "McDonald's Video Game" and "In Every Day":**  
Work that makes players *perform* the critique rather than receive it. In the McDonald's game, players manage a global fast food corporation and are inevitably pushed toward ethically questionable decisions: exploit workers, destroy rainforests, manipulate perception. The player enacts what the system requires. "In Every Day" makes players repeat the monotony of contemporary office life — given agency but only within systems that constrain action.

**Angela Vashko — "World of Crafts" intervention and "The Game":**  
Responding to misogyny in online gaming, Vashko entered a multiplayer environment not to play but to speak — engaging players in conversations about sexism, racism, and discrimination. The game space becomes a site for rehearsing difficult conversations within hostile environments. Her later work "The Game" (a dating simulator) makes players navigate conversations shaped by pickup artist rhetoric — persuasion, manipulation, control. Language itself is the system being critiqued.

**Theo Triantafyllidis — "Pastoral":**  
Deliberately suspends the logic of most digital games: no objectives, no progression system, no ethical dilemmas. The player inhabits a non-binary orc avatar and moves through quiet pastoral landscapes encountering small, absurd moments. Shifts from game to play. Resists the logic of productivity — proposing a digital experience based on presence and being-there rather than achievement.

**Mary Flanagan — Giant Joystick:**  
A joystick several meters tall, transformed into a monumental sculptural interface. Moving it requires multiple participants physically climbing and coordinating. Normal finger movements become large, collective actions. Reveals the invisible relationship between player, interface, and system — and critiques game design conventions that isolate and fragment participants.

**Jody — Modified Quake:**  
Destroy rather than enhance the visual logic of an existing first-person shooter. No walls, floors, or corridors — space becomes unrecognizable. The player moves using original controls but orientation becomes nearly impossible; the environment resembles an abstract optical illusion. The original sound remains (gunshots, footsteps, spatial audio cues) — a sensory inversion where a visually dominated genre becomes almost an acoustic navigation experience.

**Pippin Barr — Recreation of Marina Abramović; "Let's Play Ancient Greek Punishment"; "One Second Typing Tutor":**  
Barr designs games that critique game conventions. In the Abramović recreation, the player waits in a virtual queue before sitting across from the artist's avatar — waiting, anticipation, and observation are materially operated through game structure. "Let's Play Ancient Greek Punishment" forces eternal, unwinnable tasks — exposing the limits of the hero-player fantasy. "One Second Typing Tutor": the faster you type, the less time you have. Limitation rather than success.

**Le Tour d'Avignon — "Club Roadco" and "Panther Modern":**  
Collective works with no stable author behind them. In "Club Roadco," users upload selfies transformed into sculptures; the collective result emerges from ongoing communication. In "Panther Modern," users download the gallery as a file, modify it, insert their own works, modify the space — the artwork becomes an invitation to upload, edit, and present. The narrative of the exhibition changes through interaction with unseen others whose traces appear in the space.

## Key Concepts

**Ergodic Structure:** A narrative requiring non-trivial effort and active traversal to unfold; the user must do work beyond simply reading or watching. Coined by Espen Aarseth.

**Database Logic (Lev Manovich):** Digital systems store elements as collections accessed in multiple ways — meaning no longer emerges from a predetermined sequence but from paths, choices, and navigation.

**Subjunctive State (Weinbren):** The viewer's awareness that another choice would have produced a different outcome — destabilizing narrative inevitability.

**Game vs. Play:** Games are defined by rules, goals, and measurable outcomes. Play is open-ended and self-justifying. The tension between them is where game art operates critically.

**Net Art's Political Infrastructure:** The internet was never neutral. Every interactive work made on network infrastructure operates within protocols and platforms designed for control — which makes the decision to use them for non-linear, participatory meaning-making a political act.

## Key Takeaways

- **Linear narrative concentrated authorial power.** Its history is a history of who gets to define meaning — and interactive forms challenge that concentration directly
- **Form is politics.** From slow-loading hyperlinks to unnavigable 3D space, the formal choices of interactive work carry ideological content — not as message but as structure
- **Interactivity is never innocent.** Every interactive system embeds architectures of control, whether visible (game rules) or invisible (browser monopoly, database protocol). The artists surveyed here make those architectures legible
- **Database logic changes everything about how meaning is made.** When structure is replaced by collection, and sequence by navigation, the author's role fundamentally shifts
- **Games are laboratories of agency — and of its limits.** They simulate freedom while operating within coded architectures. Game art uses this condition to ask harder questions about agency, responsibility, and who decides the rules
- **Play is political.** Against the productivity logic of achievement-based gaming and digital capitalism, works like Triantafyllidis's "Pastoral" propose presence and being rather than completion as a value

## Reflection Questions

1. **Choice in your practice:** How could you incorporate meaningful choice into your work? Would you be offering genuine agency, critiquing the illusion of choice, or deliberately refusing interactivity? What does each position mean for your viewer?

2. **Form as metaphor:** Lialina used slow internet loading as a metaphor for communication breakdown after war. Are there technical constraints or qualities in your medium that could function as integral metaphors rather than problems to solve?

3. **Database vs. sequence:** Does your practice organize meaning through time (beginning, middle, end) or through a database logic (elements that can be accessed in multiple orders)? What would it change to move in the other direction?

4. **Gamification and its origins:** Game mechanics — points, levels, rewards, completion — emerged partly from military technology and research into behavioral conditioning. If you use gamification or interactive systems, what is its relationship to those origins?

5. **Hidden architectures:** What systems or platforms do you rely on for distribution, presentation, or circulation of your work? What structures of control are built into those systems? Does your work acknowledge, use, or resist them?
