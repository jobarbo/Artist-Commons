# Career Interview: Mitchell F. Chan

**Speaker:** Mitchell F. Chan — conceptual artist  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** Third and final Artist Commons session with Mitchell Chan, closing out the quarter's arc with him. The first session (114) covered his full body of work; the second (117) was a live craft workshop in Unity. This session turns to professional practice: how he built his career, navigated gallery relationships, found his collectors, and thinks about sustainability in the art world. Format mirrors the McCoy interview (112): candid, biographical, structured around questions about money, community, process, and what keeps you going.

---

## Overview

This conversation has the texture of a frank debrief with someone who has navigated multiple art world systems simultaneously — the Toronto local scene, the international crypto/NFT world, traditional gallery practice, and institutional exhibitions — and has thought carefully about why he made each move. Mitchell is direct about what he doesn't know, what surprised him, and what he'd do differently. The recurring theme: **authenticity and cadence**. Better to make one genuinely good thing annually than to produce constantly. Better to work with people you'd want to spend time with than to chase galleries that barely acknowledge you.

---

## 1. Local vs. Global — The Problem of Geography

**Canada's structural challenge:** The Canadian art world doesn't produce international "art stars" in the way that New York, London, or Berlin scenes do. Public funding exists but is disconnected from the international market. Mitchell lives and works in Toronto but has no meaningful profile there — while his work has been shown in Venice, Singapore, and Berlin.

**The "immovable feast" of the art world:** The same 60–80 people rotate through the same circuit of parties and openings in different cities — Hong Kong Art Basel, Miami Art Basel, Venice Biennale — endlessly. Breaking into that circuit from outside a major center requires patience and a different strategy than attending local openings.

**A counter-intuitive finding:** Toronto has produced genuinely important digital artists — Lorna Mills, Deaf Beef, Matt Hall and John Watkinson (CryptoPunks), Dimitri Cherniak — but most of them met each other on the internet or in New York, not locally. Geographic proximity was irrelevant.

**The internet as the real community:** For artists working in digital or technology-based mediums, online community increasingly replaces geographic community. This has a significant upside: it allows parents, artists with day jobs, and people in non-art-world cities to participate. Mitchell no longer thinks much about building a geographically local network.

**On online presence and authenticity:** Social media requires performing a curated version of yourself — which creates what he calls an "uncanny" feeling. He maintains real relationships through Zoom, email, and text, and visits people in person occasionally for genuine connection. The performance of online presence is real labor with real distortions.

---

## 2. Getting the First Gallery Shows — The Brute Force Method

Before his MFA and before his pivot to new media, Mitchell was making abstract paintings in Toronto and trying to get into galleries the hard way.

**Mentorship and the cold-email strategy:** A gallerist in Montreal (Zeke) gave him a piece of advice: *"Ask 100 times. Be prepared for 99 nos."* Mitchell took this literally.

**Execution:** He made 100 hand-crafted letterpress portfolio sleeves in record album format and physically distributed or mailed them — unsolicited, despite galleries' stated no-solicitation policies. Recipients included MoMA (he got a form letter from Ann Temkin), White Cube London, and galleries along Queen Street West in Toronto.

**First show:** A group show at Spin Gallery. The gallerists — Juno and partner — were cool and supportive. His work was hung upside down on a bathroom door. Mitchell loved it.

**Three years of walking gallery to gallery:** Queen Street West was a vibrant local scene in that period. He persistently entered increasingly better shows through sustained presence and effort.

> "Never let anyone make you feel bad for being a tryhard."

**What the work was like at the time:** He freely acknowledges the paintings were mediocre. The abstract canvases are still visible behind the pinball machine in his home studio.

---

## 3. Graduate School and the Medium Shift

**MFA at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC):** He learned, somewhat acidly, that "painting isn't real art" — there's a significant gap between art school theoretical discourse (which often dismisses traditional painting) and art world commercial practice (which sells a great deal of it).

**The useful pivot:** While in undergrad, before the MFA, he had started learning electronics, coding, robotics, and installation work through workshops at Interaccess. By grad school, he understood that his practice would move toward installation and technology-based work — work that also dovetailed with his architecture training.

**The post-MFA trap:** He moved back to Toronto immediately after graduating, severing his Chicago network. He had already noted this mistake in the first session (114) and it's worth repeating: the network built in grad school is often the primary value of the degree. Leaving disconnects you from it.

---

## 4. The Toronto Gallery Scene — Loss Leader Model

**Changed landscape:** By the time he returned, the centralized Queen Street West scene had dispersed. Galleries had moved or closed.

**Public art as bridge:** He found a sustainable entry point in public art commissions — open calls, defined budgets, transparent selection processes. Commercial work managing projects, budgets, and clients built skills he later applied to fine art. Ten-plus years of this built professional capacity that the studio practice alone couldn't have.

**Angel Gallery (Jamie Angel, director):** The first space to show his genuinely challenging, non-commercial installation work. Jamie Angel ran a program with serious curatorial ambition — and accepted that Mitchell's work would never sell.

> "I'm a loss leader. Jamie is using one month of my cutting-edge work to establish credibility, and selling beautiful oil paintings for condos the other eleven months."

He knew the arrangement and was fine with it. The cadence — producing one fine art exhibition every year or two — was exactly right: slow enough to think deeply, fast enough to get feedback and maintain momentum. He sold nothing from those shows between 2011 and 2018. That was fine; other income sources existed.

## 5. Working with Galleries and Curators

**The ambiguity of "curator":** The term means different things in different contexts.
- Commercial gallery owners are essentially small business owners using taste to select products for collectors
- Institutional curators operate differently — programming within mission and collection
- Digital art curators are still figuring out what they are

**Relationship evolution:**
- Early (Queen Street West): Transactional — gallerists hoping his work would eventually make money
- Angel Gallery: Loss leader with real benefit — exhibition cadence, feedback, the "dopamine hit" of showing work
- Present (Wyn Wah Head, DeSarth in Hong Kong): Looser arrangements
  - No exclusive contracts — he maintains independent collector relationships
  - Gallery handles visibility, networking, some sales
  - "Just figuring it out as we go along"

**On choosing who to work with:** Counter-intuitively, he factors in whether he'd want to spend time with the person. A gallery relationship that feels transactional and cold is exhausting in a different way than not having one. He also intentionally introduces gallery directors to each other — building relationships proactively before friction arises.

---

## 6. Imagining Your Ideal Viewer

**Dave:** The first serious NFT collector Mitchell met. Not formally trained in art; infinitely curious; read whitepapers and had intelligent things to say; willing to engage without condescension; genuinely funny.

Dave became Mitchell's primary internal audience — not to make work *for* him, but to ask: "Would Dave get this? Would he think it's funny and smart?"

> "I revert to 'what would Dave think?' when I'm working."

He also carries an internal committee — Nancy Baker Cahill, others — whose imagined responses he tests work against. This is his version of the McCoys' internal critic: not a single voice, but a small panel of specific people whose intelligence and seriousness he respects.

**The principle:** Having a concrete imagined viewer (not a demographic, an actual specific person) creates a real standard. The question becomes specific rather than abstract.

---

## 7. Showing Work Without Wall Text

**No explanatory text:** Mitchell's gallery works are shown without explanation. This is a major act of faith.

**The public art lesson:** 99% of people who encounter public sculpture are just catching the bus. They are not the target audience in any meaningful sense. What matters is that the work functions in layers — a surface that is inviting and accessible, a depth that rewards attention.

**Layered approach:**
- The first impression can be "wrong" — what matters is the invitation to return, to look again
- Pull the rug slowly rather than aggressively shocking from the first moment
- Don't treat viewers as dumb by explaining everything; don't treat them as hostile by withholding everything

> "Don't be an asshole to your viewer. Shock tactics without setup are lazy posturing."

**An example of this in practice:** His first video game work opens on a composition that mirrors a Winslow Homer painting exactly — giving viewers who recognize it a familiar entry point before the game's complexity unfolds. The familiar crutch earns trust; the unfamiliar follows.

## 8. How Ideas Begin

**Singular, specific triggers:** His best ideas typically begin with one specific image or line that sticks.

- *Xantar* started from a *Wayne's World* line referencing a fictional arcade game. The line was specific, unclaimed territory — and something in it sparked something real in him.
- *Digital Zones* started from recognizing that Bitcoin was simply a precise measurement of all belief in Bitcoin that existed at a given moment — which mapped exactly to Yves Klein's immaterial artwork model.

**Misunderstanding as catalyst:** He often begins by misremembering or misinterpreting another artwork — constructing a wrong version in his head. When he looks up the real work and compares, the wrong version is often better. It "passes a subliminal test" — reveals something genuinely his rather than a derivative of someone else's idea.

**Tutorial-based entry:** He starts technical projects by going through tutorials until he has a small victory — a character that moves when you press a key, a ball that falls when gravity is applied. Those small satisfactions motivate sustained learning and, crucially, give him a realistic sense of what's achievable. Rescaling ambition once you understand the tools is not failure — it's craft.

**Against the Wagnerian impulse:** Younger Mitchell wanted to make magnum opuses — full, complete, world-encompassing works. Nothing got started. He now values the harmony between tools and vision: making something he can actually finish, with the tools he actually has, at a scale that allows it to be good rather than gesturally ambitious.

## 9. On Video Games, Art, and the Difference

**The distinction that matters:**
- A video game is a system, a pastime, an escape — it distracts you from your life
- An artwork carves a perch from which you look back at the system — it helps you understand something real in the world

> "Mario is better than 99% of contemporary artwork in terms of craft and execution. But it's a hobby. An artwork *educates* — it connects you to something real."

This doesn't make games lesser. It clarifies what he's trying to do differently with game-based work: use the medium's tools to produce understanding rather than entertainment.

## 10. The Blockchain/NFT Arc

**Early entry:** *Digital Zones* was positioned for two audiences simultaneously — people in crypto trying to understand art, and curators trying to understand crypto without appearing purely capitalist. The Blue Paper (his 33-page essay documenting Klein → Bitcoin → Ethereum) was the work; the smart contract was almost incidental.

**When it worked:** The positioning was clear, rigorous, and timed well. Nobody cared at first; eventually the work ended up in Venice alongside original Yves Klein pieces and Marcel Duchamp's Monte Carlo Bonds, then Singapore and Berlin.

**Auction inconsistencies:** Two auction results — roughly \$1 million for *Digital Zone* and \$18,000 for a UNICEF charity auction piece — created a problem. The public ledger of blockchain means inconsistent prices can't be hidden. A traditional dealer (Pascal) pointed out that sustainable market positioning requires a consistent price ladder, not wild jumps.

> "These are champagne problems — better than having to explain to my wife why the car is being sold."

**Don't be shamed by numbers:** Having a significant auction result next to your name is positioning value. Auction houses are currently not good at digital art, but the promotional value of a big number often outweighs the awkwardness of the context.

## 11. Venice Biennale: The Surreal Exhibition

He received an email from Fondazione Prada while boarding a plane to Milan — thought it was a phishing scam. It was from curator Christophe Buchel (Swiss conceptual artist, described as a hermit; nobody knows what he looks like) for an exhibition concurrent with the 60th Venice Biennale, on the theme of debt, religion, and art.

**The lineup:** Duchamp (Monte Carlo Bond), Yves Klein, Andy Warhol, Joseph Beuys, Titian (loaned from the Uffizi). Chan's *Digital Zone* included.

**The presentation:** Everything was treated like thrift store objects. Titian on pegboard (antique-roadshow style). Warhol documents shipped in their original cardboard boxes, unopened. Pompeian coins in generic display cases.

**His experience:** One email asking what format he wanted (hardware wallet? iPad?). Then nothing. He wasn't officially invited to the opening. Zero input on presentation.

> "Nothing to be learned. Completely surreal. I wouldn't allow that again unless it turned out to be the best exhibition I was ever in. Which it was."

## 12. Sustainability — Practical Advice

**Get a job. Don't be ashamed:**  
Economic independence means not making art out of desperation. Art made from desperation usually fails — and it's hard to recover from a body of work produced under that pressure. Starbucks, teaching, commercial work — whatever it takes.

> "Economically independent means you're not making art because you need the money. Art out of desperation usually fails."

**Time is the best teacher:**  
He made uneven work for ten-plus years before making his first genuinely good thing. Staying in the practice — keeping the right to keep going — is itself the work.

**Delusional confidence helps:**  
When young, you don't know how bad you are yet. That's an asset. The confidence lets you start; the work teaches you what you still need to learn.

**The cadence model:**  
Six months of studio work → exhibition → feedback and the "dopamine hit" → back to studio. This rhythm beats the "cocktail party" life of constant networking. As you advance, you attend fewer parties — the novelty wears off and you'd rather be with friends or in the studio.

**On selling out:**  
He doesn't use the term moralizingly. Gen X could afford "damn the man"; millennials and Gen Z often cannot. The question is whether you can find areas of resistance within commercial work — whether there are things you won't do, positions you maintain, even while participating in commercial systems. Self-questioning within the work is healthy; self-righteous refusal can be a luxury.

## Key Takeaways

- **The "tryhard" label is a compliment, not an insult.** Brute force — letterpress portfolios, cold emails, gallery to gallery on foot — is how you start when you have no connections
- **Loss leader status can be the right model.** Knowing what you are to a gallery — and being fine with it because the cadence serves you — is better than chasing a position you don't have
- **Imagining a specific viewer is more useful than imagining a demographic.** A concrete person — someone whose intelligence and seriousness you know — gives you a real standard to work against
- **Layer your work; trust your viewer.** The first impression can be wrong; what matters is the invitation to return. Don't explain everything; don't shock without earning it
- **The best ideas are specific and slightly misremembered.** Generic ambition produces nothing; the off-kilter, specific, slightly-wrong idea is often more yours than the correct reference would be
- **The cadence matters more than output level.** One genuinely good thing per year, in a rhythm of studio → exhibition → feedback, beats constant production
- **Economic independence is an artistic condition.** Not a luxury — a precondition for making work that isn't desperate
- **The art world is learnable.** Its rules are discoverable; its gatekeepers are people; its systems are navigable with patience and genuine work

## Reflection Questions

1. **The ideal viewer:** Who is your equivalent of Dave — a specific, concrete person whose imagined response gives you a real standard? If you can't name someone, what would that person be like?

2. **Cadence vs. output:** What would it mean to design your practice around cadence — one genuinely good show every one or two years — rather than around constant production? What would you stop doing?

3. **Getting over yourself:** Where are you holding onto a vision of your practice that doesn't match what the work actually requires? What would "getting over yourself" look like in your current project?

4. **The loss leader question:** What is your current relationship with the gallery, institution, or platform you're working with? Is it transactional, supportive, or unclear? Does your status within that relationship serve your actual needs — even if not in the obvious way?

5. **Desperation vs. independence:** What is the relationship between your economic situation and the work you're making right now? Are you making work because you need it financially, or because you need it artistically? What would change if you separated those two motivations?
