# Building a Long-Term Career in the Arts: Candid Interview with Jenn and Kevin McCoy

**Speakers:** Jenn McCoy, Kevin McCoy — artists  
**Host:** Haiver — Artist Commons director  
**Context:** Third and final Artist Commons session with the McCoys, closing out the program's first five-week cycle. The first session covered their body of work; the second focused on craft and process. This session turns to professional practice: how to build and sustain a long-term career as an artist.

---

## Overview

This conversation has the texture of a long debrief between trusted colleagues. Jenn and Kevin speak candidly — about money, identity, collaboration, community-building, failure, and what it means to still be going after 30+ years. There is no magic formula here, and they're the first to say so. What emerges instead is a set of principles, hard-won from experience, about how to set up a life that makes sustained creative work possible.

---

## 1. Deciding You're an Artist

Neither Jenn nor Kevin began with a clear plan. Both came from working-class backgrounds (schoolteacher parents, no inherited wealth) and arrived at art-making through sideways paths — Jenn through experimental film, Kevin through philosophy.

**Kevin:** The transition into the identity of *"I am an artist"* took years and was the harder thing. The biggest challenge is believing it yourself — quieting the internal voice that says you're fooling yourself.

> "It's kind of like coming out of the closet. 'I'm an artist. This is what I do.' And the biggest challenge is believing that yourself."

Once the identity is committed to, it becomes more durable. You can be in a dry spell, in the hospital, in a down cycle — and still be an artist. The identity doesn't depend on current output.

**Jenn:** There was no realistic view of what was ahead. They survived on energy. One year, she made \$6,000 and was fine. "If you have okay health and you're willing to eat badly and live in a weird apartment — we did that for a really long time."

---

## 2. Freeing Up Time to Make Work

**Control your living expenses.** The single most important financial move is keeping fixed costs low — choosing where to live, how to live. Every dollar of overhead you eliminate is time returned to you.

**Teaching as the day job.** For the McCoys, the natural fit was academia. An MFA (the terminal degree in their field) opened the door to university teaching — demanding and poorly paid, but structurally compatible with studio practice. It's not a panacea, but it was their path.

**The day job and the practice feeding each other.** Jenn prefers work that connects to her thinking rather than compartmentalizing completely. Teaching, web design, interactive design — things that "feed the practice." She's skeptical of the pure compartmentalization approach, though she acknowledges it works for some. For artists with strong technology skills, well-paid tech work can fund a part-time practice — but the risk is getting swallowed by deadlines and ship dates.

**Collaboration as structure.** The McCoys' collaboration is partly a practical solution: if one is at the day job, the other keeps the studio practice alive. It's not a zero-sum competition for studio time. By design, it breaks down the barrier between art and life.

---

## 3. Drawing from the Same Well

When the day job and the art practice draw on the same kind of creative thinking, there's a real risk of exhaustion.

**Kevin's answer:** Art-making and creative-technology work have different endpoints and different goals — they're not actually the same act. Giving yourself permission to let yourself off the hook when things are dry is not failure. The identity holds even when production doesn't.

The more fundamental challenge is the internal critic. Getting past it is the real work.

---

## 4. Collaboration and the Artist Relationship

The McCoys designed their collaboration specifically to remove the barrier between art and life. The practical upshot: no zero-sum competition for studio time, and no separate world where the other person doesn't understand what you're working on.

**The risk:** Under deadline pressure, there's nowhere to hide. Stress bleeds into the relationship. Their mitigation: separate teaching jobs, separate social lives, and — borrowing a rule from managing any close relationship — not taking yourself too seriously and giving ground.

**A structural downside Jenn names:** In periods of intense production (they describe 2001–2008, with three galleries and non-stop art fairs), there wasn't space to pause and ask whether every idea leaving the studio was the right one. The harder, weirder ideas sometimes need more internal momentum than a collaborative calendar allows. The correction: build in regular reflection time *before* the deadline crunch.

---

## 5. Building Community and Getting Your First Gallery

The McCoys arrived in New York with one project and started from almost zero.

**The fax era equivalent:** They literally faxed open studio invitations to strangers — curators, museum people, anyone they'd heard of. One knot of friends showed up. One of them ran an artist-run gallery on the floor above their studio. That show led to a review. That led to a collector. That led to Postmasters noticing them at an SVA group show.

**The "three times" rule:**  
> "If someone hears your name from three different people, three different times, they start to go: this is work I should pay attention to."

Their approach: figure out how many nights a week you can possibly go out and still get studio work done — then do both at that rate. Show up at openings. Meet people. But the work has to be solid. You can't just network; the networking only activates if the work is there to back it up.

**The digital art community advantage:** The field was small enough that you could actually meet most of the people in it. Jenn's observation: painters compete in a much larger field; the weirdness of tech-based art creates a different social dynamic — more exchange, more "how did you do that?", less competitive edge.

**Key names in their early community:** Ricardo Dominguez (connector), Rhizome (Jenn served on an early board), Corey Arcangel, Mark Napier, MTAA, Wolfgang Staehle — a loose group of artists meeting in the then-nascent digital art scene, at freezing openings, listservs, meeting groups.

---

## 6. The First Real Gallery: Postmasters

Postmasters Gallery (New York) became the McCoys' primary gallery for decades. How it started:

- Magda and Tamash at Postmasters were interested in art and technology — but also showed painters, sculptors, a genuinely eclectic program. The conversation felt alive and intellectually spirited
- The first opportunity was a "project space" — literally a two-foot square hole in the poured concrete floor of the gallery

Their response: they made a **miniature functional elevator** for the hole in the floor. It opened, spoke, delivered phrases from Philip K. Dick's *Valis* — a neoplatonist appendix about the nature of mind and reality. It was ridiculous, funny, worked just barely enough, cost money they didn't have, and required them to manually trigger steps from the basement during the opening.

It completely captivated people. It led to an *Artforum* profile.

> "You get an opportunity. You commit to your practice. When an opportunity comes, you just maximize it — not in some gross networking sense, but: I'm going to put the best thinking I can into this. I'm not going to hold back at a practical or creative or intellectual level."

**Show up for people who support you.** Jenn's corollary: fill your career working with people who are delightful and supportive. Don't chase galleries or institutions that barely give you the time of day. There's no brain surgeon logic here — art is culture and conversation. Spend your time with the people who are actually engaged.

---

## 7. Documentation as Its Own Practice

For artists making complex, time-based, installation, or technology-based work — documentation is not optional and not simple.

**What documentation actually includes** (from their recent Whitney Museum sale):
- Earlier versions of the code
- Current code
- Installation shots from multiple previous exhibitions showing optimal lighting and space
- A floor plan diagram for optimal layout
- Component specs for electronics (in case of future replacements)
- A video showing what correct output looks like (so future installers know what "right" means)
- Backup video option in case software fails during display

This is sometimes called a **stewardship packet** — designed to outlast the artist's direct involvement, guiding future installations and preserving intent across institutional turnovers.

**Kevin's note on scale:** The documentation surface area keeps expanding (do you have a 3D walkthrough?). Do your best, stay organized, but don't let managing documentation become an excuse to avoid making work. There are actual media conservation professionals now — let them solve some problems.

> "A really good-sounding reason for why you're not making your work is 'I'm managing my archive to professionalize it.' Sure. But why don't you just make some new work?"

---

## 8. Talking About the Work Over Time

**The noble lie of the slide lecture:** Looking back across 30 years of work, you narrate it as if it were all inevitable and organized. In truth, Kevin says: "It was all just chaos and fear and ignorance."

**The one or two ideas:** Artists tend to only have one or two real ideas, which keep taking different forms. The McCoys see this not as a limitation but as a through line — the same preoccupations, new containers.

**Talking about technology-based work years later:** The harder the technology was to describe in its moment, the harder it is to explain historically. Jenn's solution: **stick to the conceptual development** of the work, not the technology. The technology was incidental — a tool. If the idea was strong, it holds regardless of whether the specific platform or system is still legible.

**Being skeptical of the breathless new:** The McCoys reflect that their work has been consistently skeptical of technological utopianism, which has made it more durable. Work that was earnestly excited about specific technologies tends to age poorly when those technologies reveal their darker dimensions.

---

## 9. Getting to the Idea (For Artists Working with Technology)

One of the most common traps for tech-based artists: becoming obsessed with the tool, then trying to retrofit meaning.

**Jenn's answer: personal connection, not illustration.**  
Ask yourself: *What is your personal relationship to this project?* If the work is just an illustration of a new piece of technology, someone else can do that. What is *your* interest? Where does it come from? A personal connection makes the work more connected to you, more connected to your future trajectory, and less like a one-off experiment.

> "People love to look at work where they can see the connection from the artist to the work."

Yayoi Kusama as a model: one idea (the dot, the infinity, the net), pursued with obsessive consistency from 1950 to 1997. Personal obsession is future-proof in a way that technology adoption is not.

**Kevin's answer: put down the phone, pay attention to your actual reality.**  
Being very online is a trap. What is *your* reality today — not the latest AI model, not what Hollywood is worried about, not the agent swarm debate? Go outside. Pay close attention, in minute detail, to what you actually see. Find the sensorial encounter that you, specifically, can frame and bring to an audience.

> "The scientists are creating functions. The philosophers are creating concepts. And the artists are creating sensations. So what sensorial aggregate are you bringing in response to this thing that you are seeing?"

Even if your practice is entirely digital — it's still about a sensation-based encounter you are framing for other people. The tool is incidental to that.

## 10. Scoping, Budgeting, and Producing Complex Work

**Kevin's approach: production schematic thinking** — the ability to envision a full production chain (A → B → C → D) and map what each stage requires. This comes partly from Jenn's film background, where production methodology and segmentation are built in.

**Backfilling the timeline:** Start with the date and the image in your mind of what you want to exist at that moment. Then work backwards: what needs to happen, in what order, over what timeframe? What's the big lift that requires bringing in an outside person?

**Fake vs. real functionality:** A key artistic hack. Ask: does this need to *actually* function, or does it need to *present the semblance of function*? The experience of functionality can be the story. The elevator worked just barely enough — sometimes with manual triggering from the basement. That was sufficient.

---

## 11. Grants and Funding

**Jenn's key insight: make the work, then write the grant.**  
The common wisdom is: write the grant, wait to see if you get it, then make the work. The McCoys' approach is the reverse. Start the project. As you work, apply for funding. By the time you hear back, the project is done. If you get the grant, you pay yourself back or fund the next project. If you don't, you still have a finished piece.

> "You can always be in a cycle, kind of behind money coming in the door."

**Other grant realities:**  
- Grantors rarely check whether you made exactly what you said you'd make. What matters is that the money was spent on a project and you can justify how your research evolved
- Funders don't guard your work jealously — they want it to have more reach. Put them in dialogue with each other. A grant organization helped fund a catalog for a museum show even though they were different organizations. Creative Capital bought a projector when the McCoys got into Sundance and the venue didn't have one
- Research phases absolutely can be funded; you just have to make a plausible case for where you're going

---

## 12. Iterating on a Body of Work

The McCoys would have stopped making their database cinema pieces after the first one. Postmasters told them not to.

> "Just because you've thought it through and feel done with it — not everybody has caught on yet."

The gallery pushed them to make pieces that were similar but more refined, more beautiful, with different content. The result: a body of work, not a single piece. A recognizable practice.

**The principle:** Slow down. Give people multiple bites at the apple. The artwork is a **carrier for ideas** — you can make many different projects more cheaply by staying within the same form factor rather than reinventing everything each time. Each new iteration extends reach without requiring you to start from scratch.

**The corollary for authenticity:** This only works if you can make more versions that still feel genuine and evolved to you. The McCoys found they could — and that it was artistically productive, not just commercial.

---

## 13. What Success Means Now

**Kevin:** The goal is to keep doing it. He became interested in artists in their 80s who were still going. The glass-half-full keeps winning, against all reasons it shouldn't. He's still waiting for the piece where it all comes together — and still believes it's coming.

> "I want it all to come together. I want the earth to just say: yes, there it is. That thing."

**Jenn:** More optimistic about her own capacity to keep working than about predicting where the art world is going. The categories — high vs. low, digital vs. physical — are blurring faster than anyone can track. The goal: stay curious. Keep generating new curiosity.

> "Try to keep being curious about things."

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Commit to the identity first.** Believing you're an artist is the hardest and most important step. The output level varies; the identity holds
- **Control your overhead.** Every dollar of living expense you eliminate is time returned to your practice
- **You have to do both.** Show up in the community *and* make solid work. One without the other doesn't work
- **The "three times" rule.** Getting your name mentioned by different people to the same gatekeeper is how you become visible. Show up
- **Max out every opportunity.** Not as career strategy — as commitment to doing the best thinking you can with what you've been given
- **Show up for people who support you.** Don't chase those who aren't interested; work with people who light up around your work
- **Documentation is its own project.** Treat it seriously. A stewardship packet outlasts you and makes the work survivable
- **Stick to the concept, not the technology.** Future-proof your ability to talk about the work by grounding it in ideas, not platforms
- **Personal connection over illustration.** If the work is just demonstrating a tool, someone else can do that. What's *your* connection to this?
- **Make the work, then write the grant.** Don't wait for funding to start. Apply while you build
- **Slow down on bodies of work.** Give ideas multiple containers before moving on. The artwork is a carrier for the idea — you can make more of them

---

## Reflection Questions

1. **Identity:** What would it mean to commit fully to the identity of "I am an artist" — not dependent on output level, exhibition status, or external validation? What would change in how you work?

2. **The well:** If your day job and your art practice draw from similar thinking, how do you protect creative energy for the studio? What structures could help?

3. **Community:** Where are you showing up that puts your name in circulation? Who are the three to five people in your field who, if they each mentioned your name to someone different, would start to create awareness?

4. **The opportunity:** What's the last opportunity you had (however small) that you held back on — didn't fully commit your best thinking to? What would "maxing it out" have looked like?

5. **The body of work:** Is there an idea or form you've explored once and moved away from because you felt done with it — but others haven't fully had a chance to encounter it yet? What would a second or third iteration look like?
